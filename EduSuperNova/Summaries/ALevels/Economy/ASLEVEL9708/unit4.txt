THE MACROECONOMY

NATIONAL INCOME STATISTICS

 GROSS DOMESTIC PRODUCT (GDP)
    Definition: The total market value of all final goods and services
    produced within a country's borders in a specific time period.

    * KEY RESTRAINTS:
      - FINAL GOODS ONLY: Excludes intermediate goods to avoid "Double
        Counting."
      - GEOGRAPHIC: Produced WITHIN the country (e.g., a foreign car
        plant in Germany counts toward German GDP).
      - EXCLUSIONS: Used goods (resale), illegal/black market activity,
        and home-produced goods (e.g., DIY).

 NOMINAL VS. REAL GDP
    This is a critical distinction for evaluating actual economic growth.

    - NOMINAL GDP: Measured at CURRENT prices. It can rise just because
      prices went up (inflation), even if production stayed the same.
    - REAL GDP: Measured at CONSTANT (Base Year) prices. It removes the
      effect of inflation to show actual changes in output.

    * THE GDP DEFLATOR: A price index used to adjust Nominal GDP to Real.
      Formula: (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) x 100

 MEASURING GDP: THE EXPENDITURE APPROACH
    GDP is the sum of spending by four main sectors:
    Formula: GDP = C + I + G + (X - M)

    - C (CONSUMPTION): Household spending on durable/non-durable goods.
    - I (INVESTMENT): Firm spending on capital (machinery/factories)
      and household spending on NEW housing.
    - G (GOVERNMENT SPENDING): State spending on public services
      (health, education, defense).
    - NX (NET EXPORTS): Exports (X) minus Imports (M).
      * If X > M = Trade Surplus (Adds to GDP).
      * If M > X = Trade Deficit (Subtracts from GDP).

 GNI AND NNI (NATIONAL VS. DOMESTIC)
    - GROSS NATIONAL INCOME (GNI): Measures income earned by a country's
      residents, regardless of where they are in the world.
      Formula: GNI = GDP + Net Income from Abroad.

    - NET NATIONAL INCOME (NNI): Adjusts GNI for the "wear and tear"
      of machines and infrastructure.
      Formula: NNI = GNI - Depreciation (Capital Consumption).

 AS LEVEL EXAM TIP: REAL GDP PER CAPITA
    To compare the "Standard of Living" between countries, we use
    Real GDP per Capita.
    Formula: Real GDP / Total Population.
    This tells us how much income is available, on average, per person.


THE CIRCULAR FLOW OF INCOME


 THE SIMPLE TWO-SECTOR MODEL (CLOSED ECONOMY)
    This model assumes only two entities: Households and Firms.

    - HOUSEHOLDS: Own the factors of production (Land, Labor, Capital,
      Enterprise). They provide these to firms in exchange for Income (Y).
    - FIRMS: Use factors to produce goods and services. They pay
      households wages, rent, interest, and profit.
    - THE FLOW: Households spend all their income on goods produced by
      firms (Consumption).
    - KEY IDENTITY: National Output = National Income = National Expenditure.

 THE OPEN ECONOMY MODEL (FIVE SECTORS)
    In reality, money leaves the "inner flow" (Consumption) and new
    money enters it from other sectors.

    A. LEAKAGES / WITHDRAWALS (W)
    Money that leaves the circular flow, reducing the size of National Income.
        1. SAVINGS (S): Money put into financial institutions (banks)
           rather than spent.
        2. TAXES (T): Money paid to the government (Net Taxes = Taxes
           paid minus transfer payments received).
        3. IMPORTS (M): Money spent on goods produced in other countries.

    B. INJECTIONS (J)
        Money that enters the circular flow from outside the inner loop.
        1. INVESTMENT (I): Spending by firms on capital goods using
           funds from financial markets.
        2. GOVERNMENT SPENDING (G): State spending on goods and services.
        3. EXPORTS (X): Money coming into the domestic economy from
           foreign buyers.

 MACROECONOMIC EQUILIBRIUM
    The economy is in equilibrium when total Injections equal total
    Withdrawals:
    Formula: J = W  =>  (I + G + X) = (S + T + M)

    * IF J > W (Injections exceed Withdrawals):
      - National Income (Y) will RISE.
      - Economic growth occurs as firms increase production.
    * IF W > J (Withdrawals exceed Injections):
      - National Income (Y) will FALL.
      - The economy enters a contraction/recession.

 AS LEVEL EXAM TIP: THE THREE WAYS TO MEASURE GDP
    Because of the circular flow, we can measure a country's wealth in
    three ways that should theoretically give the same result:
    1. THE OUTPUT METHOD: Value of all goods produced.
    2. THE INCOME METHOD: Total of all wages, rents, interest, and profits.
    3. THE EXPENDITURE METHOD: Total spending (C + I + G + [X-M]).

AGGREGATE DEMAND AND AGGREGATE SUPPLY (AD-AS)

 AGGREGATE DEMAND (AD)
    AD is the total spending on all domestically produced goods and
    services in an economy at a given price level.

    - FORMULA: AD = C + I + G + (X - M)
    - THE CURVE: Slopes downward. A change in the Price Level (PL)
      causes a MOVEMENT along the curve (Expansion/Contraction).

    - SHIFTS IN AD: Occur when C, I, G, or (X-M) change due to factors
      OTHER than the price level.
      * RIGHT SHIFT: Higher consumer confidence, lower taxes, or
        increased government spending.
      * LEFT SHIFT: Higher interest rates (reduces C and I), or a
        recession in a major trading partner (reduces X).

 SHORT-RUN AGGREGATE SUPPLY (SRAS)
    SRAS is the total output producers are willing to supply at different
    price levels, assuming factor prices (like wages) are FIXED.

    - THE CURVE: Slopes upward. Producers increase output when prices
      rise because profit margins expand in the short term.
    - SHIFTS IN SRAS: Caused by changes in the COSTS OF PRODUCTION.
      * RIGHT SHIFT: Lower oil prices, decrease in wages, or business
        subsidies.
      * LEFT SHIFT: Increase in raw material costs, higher minimum wage,
        or indirect taxes.

 MACROECONOMIC EQUILIBRIUM

    Equilibrium occurs where AD = SRAS. This determines the equilibrium
    Price Level (PL) and Real Output (Y).

    * DEMAND-SIDE SHOCK: If AD shifts right, both PL and Y rise
      (Demand-pull inflation).
    * SUPPLY-SIDE SHOCK: If SRAS shifts left, PL rises and Y falls.
      This is called STAGFLATION (Stagnation + Inflation).

 LONG-RUN AGGREGATE SUPPLY (LRAS)
    In the long run, the economy's capacity depends on the quantity and
    quality of resources (factors of production).

    A. KEYNESIAN PERSPECTIVE:
    - The curve is horizontal at low levels of output (spare capacity).
    - It becomes vertical at full employment (Ymax) because the economy
      has run out of resources.


    B. NEW CLASSICAL PERSPECTIVE:
    - The curve is a VERTICAL line.
    - In the long run, the economy always returns to its "Potential
      Output," regardless of the price level.

 SHIFTING THE LRAS (ECONOMIC GROWTH)
    The LRAS only shifts if the productive POTENTIAL of the country
    changes.
    - FACTORS: Technological advances, improved education/skills,
      discovery of new resources, or increased labor productivity.
    - NOTE: A rightward shift in LRAS represents long-term economic
      growth.

ECONOMIC GROWTH

 DEFINING ECONOMIC GROWTH

    Economic growth is a sustained increase in the real output of an
    economy over time.

    - ACTUAL GROWTH: An increase in Real GDP (moving from a point inside
      the PPC to a point closer to the boundary).
    - POTENTIAL GROWTH: An increase in the economy’s capacity to produce
      (shifting the PPC outward or shifting the LRAS to the right).

 MEASURING GROWTH

    Growth is measured as the percentage change in Real GDP.

    - FORMULA (Real GDP Growth):
      [(Real GDP Year 2 - Real GDP Year 1) / Real GDP Year 1] x 100

    - REAL GDP PER CAPITA: The most important measure for "Living
      Standards." It accounts for population size.
      Formula: Real GDP / Total Population
      * NOTE: If the population grows faster than Real GDP, the standard
        of living (per capita income) actually falls.

 DETERMINANTS OF LABOR PRODUCTIVITY

    Productivity is the output per worker per hour. Growth depends on
    making labor more effective through:

    1. PHYSICAL CAPITAL: More and better machinery, equipment, and
       infrastructure (Investment).
    2. HUMAN CAPITAL: Improvements in the skills, education, and
       health of the workforce.
    3. TECHNOLOGICAL ADVANCEMENT: Innovation, R&D, and the
       application of new processes (e.g., AI, automation).

 INSTITUTIONAL FACTORS
    For growth to occur, the "Economic Environment" must be stable:
    - PROPERTY RIGHTS: Legal protection that encourages firms to invest.
    - POLITICAL STABILITY: Reduces risk for domestic and foreign investors.
    - INFRASTRUCTURE: Efficient transport and communication networks.
    - EDUCATION/HEALTH: Ensuring a capable and reliable workforce.

 AS LEVEL EXAM TIP: PPC VS. LRAS
    You can show economic growth using two different diagrams:
    - PPC DIAGRAM: An outward shift of the curve.
    - AD-AS DIAGRAM: A rightward shift of the LRAS curve.
    Both represent an increase in the "Potential Output" (Full Employment)
    of the economy.

UNEMPLOYMENT

 KEY DEFINITIONS & FORMULAS
    - WORKING-AGE POPULATION: Typically residents aged 15+.
    - LABOR FORCE: The total number of people either working (Employed)
      or actively seeking work (Unemployed).
    - UNEMPLOYED: Those without a job who are BOTH available to work
      and actively seeking employment.

    * FORMULAS:
      1. LABOR FORCE = Employed + Unemployed
      2. UNEMPLOYMENT RATE = (Unemployed / Labor Force) x 100
      3. LABOR FORCE PARTICIPATION RATE (LFPR) =
         (Labor Force / Working-Age Population) x 100
      4. EMPLOYMENT-TO-POPULATION RATIO =
         (Employed / Working-Age Population) x 100

 LIMITATIONS OF UNEMPLOYMENT STATISTICS
    Official records often underestimate the true scale of the problem:
    - DISCOURAGED WORKERS: "Hidden unemployed" who have given up
      searching and are thus no longer counted in the labor force.
    - UNDEREMPLOYMENT: Statistics do not distinguish between
      full-time and part-time workers.
    - INFORMAL ECONOMY: People working "off the books" may be
      counted as unemployed despite having income.

 TYPES OF UNEMPLOYMENT
    A. NATURAL UNEMPLOYMENT: The level of unemployment that exists even
       when the labor market is in equilibrium (Full Employment).
       * FRICTIONAL: Short-term; people moving between jobs or
         entering the workforce (e.g., recent graduates).
       * STRUCTURAL: Long-term; caused by a mismatch between workers'
         skills and the skills required by new technologies or industries.

    B. UNNATURAL / EXCESS UNEMPLOYMENT:
       * CYCLICAL (Demand-Deficient): Caused by a recession or a
         fall in Aggregate Demand (AD).
       * SEASONAL: Linked to certain times of year (Tourism, Agriculture).
       * TECHNOLOGICAL: Result of automation or mechanization
         replacing human labor.

 THE ROLE OF WAGES
    - MINIMUM WAGES: If set above the equilibrium market price, they
      create a surplus of labor (Unemployment).
    - EFFICIENCY WAGES: High wages paid by firms to increase
      productivity and reduce worker turnover.

 CONSEQUENCES OF UNEMPLOYMENT
    - ECONOMIC:
      * LOSS OF OUTPUT: Actual GDP falls below Potential GDP.
      * FISCAL COST: Increased government spending on benefits and
        lower tax revenue (Income Tax/VAT).
    - SOCIAL/INDIVIDUAL:
      * LOSS OF HUMAN CAPITAL: Skills fade during long-term
        unemployment ("Hysteresis").
      * SOCIAL INSTABILITY: Increased poverty and political tension.

 STATISTICS & GROUPS (COVID-19 CASE STUDY)
    The pandemic highlighted how unemployment is unequally distributed:
    - MINORITIES & YOUTH: Often the first to be laid off in a crisis.
    - GENDER GAP: Women were disproportionately affected due to
      increases in unpaid childcare duties during lockdowns.
    - RECOVERY STATS: Between 1999 and 2016, technology replaced
      routine jobs but created over 23 million new jobs in Europe.

PRICE STABILITY: INFLATION AND DEFLATION

 KEY DEFINITIONS
    - INFLATION: A sustained increase in the general price level over
      time, leading to a fall in the purchasing power of money.
    - DEFLATION: A decrease in the general price level.
    - HYPERINFLATION: Extreme inflation (over 50% per month) where
      money loses its value almost entirely, often leading to barter.

    * TYPES OF INFLATION:
      - Creeping: Low and stable (around 2%). Generally considered
        healthy for an economy.
      - Galloping: High rates (5% - 10%+). Discourages saving and
        domestic investment.

 MEASURING INFLATION: CONSUMER PRICE INDEX (CPI)
    The CPI tracks the price changes of a "representative basket" of
    goods and services bought by a typical household.

    * CALCULATION STEPS:
      1. Select the Basket: Based on household spending patterns.
      2. Weighting: Assigning importance to items (e.g., Housing has
         a higher weight than Bread).
      3. Price Collection: Monthly data gathering.

    * FORMULAS:
      - CPI = (Cost of basket in current year / Cost in base year) x 100
      - INFLATION RATE = [(CPI Year 2 - CPI Year 1) / CPI Year 1] x 100

 LIMITATIONS (BIASES) OF CPI
    - NEW PRODUCT BIAS: Slow to include new tech (e.g., streaming).
    - QUALITY BIAS: Price rises may reflect better quality, not
      just inflation.
    - SUBSTITUTION BIAS: Consumers switch to cheaper alternatives
      when prices rise, which the fixed basket may not reflect.

 CAUSES OF INFLATION
    A. DEMAND-PULL INFLATION:
       - Caused by excessive Aggregate Demand (AD) shifting right.
       - "Too much money chasing too few goods."
       - Common in booming economies or due to high Govt spending.

    B. COST-PUSH INFLATION:
       - Caused by rising costs of production (wages, raw materials,
         energy).
       - Shifts the SRAS curve to the LEFT.
       - Leads to "Stagflation" (Rising prices + Falling output).

    C. MONETARIST VIEW:
       - Inflation is caused by the money supply growing faster than
         the production of goods and services.

 CONSEQUENCES OF INFLATION
    * NEGATIVE:
      - REDISTRIBUTION: Harms fixed-income earners (pensioners) and
        lenders; benefits borrowers (as the real value of debt falls).
      - UNCERTAINTY: Firms may stop investing if they can't predict
        future costs/prices.
      - MENU COSTS: The cost to firms of physically changing price
        tags and menus.
      - SHOE-LEATHER COSTS: Time spent searching for better prices as
        money loses value.

    * POSITIVE (Moderate Inflation):
      - Can encourage production if prices rise faster than costs.
      - Reduces the real burden of national and private debt.
