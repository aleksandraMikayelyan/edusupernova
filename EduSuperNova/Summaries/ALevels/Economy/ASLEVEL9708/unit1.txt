THE FUNDAMENTAL ECONOMIC PROBLEM: SCARCITY
The central economic problem is the conflict between UNLIMITED WANTS
and FINITE (SCARCE) RESOURCES.
- Scarcity necessitates CHOICE.
- Every choice involves an OPPORTUNITY COST.

THE THREE FUNDAMENTAL QUESTIONS
Every economy must answer these three questions:
    - WHAT TO PRODUCE? (Prioritizing goods/services based on needs).
    - HOW TO PRODUCE? (Choosing between Labor-intensive vs. Capital-intensive).
    - FOR WHOM TO PRODUCE? (Deciding how the total output is distributed).

FACTORS OF PRODUCTION (RESOURCES)
Resources are combined in the PROCESS OF PRODUCTION to create output.
Each factor has a specific "reward" (return):

    LAND: All natural resources (fields, mineral deposits, water).
      - REWARD: Rent.
    LABOUR: Human effort, both physical and mental.
      - REWARD: Wages or Salaries.
    CAPITAL: Man-made aids to production (machinery, tools, technology).
      - REWARD: Interest.
    ENTERPRISE (Entrepreneur): The individual who organizes the other
      factors and takes the risk of production.
      - REWARD: Profit.


PRODUCTION VS. CONSUMPTION
    - PRODUCTION: The process of combining resources to create goods/services
      with the "utility" (capacity) to satisfy wants.
    - CONSUMPTION: The act of using goods/services to satisfy wants.
    - CONSUMER DURABLES: Goods that provide utility over a long period
      (e.g., cars, washing machines).

OPPORTUNITY COST
    The cost of any choice expressed in terms of the "next best alternative
    forgone." It is not just about money, but about what you give up.

PRODUCTION TRENDS
    - DEVELOPED ECONOMIES: Often move toward the "Tertiary Sector" (Services).
    - DEVELOPING ECONOMIES: Often rely more on the "Primary Sector"
      (Agriculture/Extraction).
    - Structural changes usually occur as a country's standard of living rises.

ECONOMIC SYSTEMS AND RESOURCE ALLOCATION

An economic system is defined as the means by which choices are made regarding resource management.
It includes the institutions and mechanisms that direct the production, consumption, and distribution of goods and services.

Economists distinguish between four main economic systems:

TRADITIONAL ECONOMY
The traditional economy is the oldest type of economic system.
It is characterised by manual labour, a minimal division of labour, and underdeveloped infrastructure.

    -ADVANTAGES: Minimal wastage and high sustainability, as communities typically consume only what they produce.
    -DISADVANTAGES: Limited economic progress and development due to a lack of labour specialisation and modern technology.

COMMAND (CENTRALLY PLANNED) ECONOMY
In this system, the state plays the central role in decision-making.
The government determines what is produced, how it is produced, and sets both prices and wages.

    -ADVANTAGES: Essential public services, such as education and healthcare,
    are often provided free of charge to the citizens.
    -DISADVANTAGES: A disconnect between production and actual consumer needs often leads to surpluses
    of unwanted goods and shortages of essential ones, creating significant underlying economic imbalances.

FREE MARKET ECONOMY
In a free market, individuals and private firms own the factors of production.
Economic decisions are driven by self-interest with minimal to no government interference.

    MARKET MECHANISM: This coordinates the plans of buyers and producers through the forces of supply and demand.
    While firms aim for profit, individuals seek to maximise value.
    PRICE: This serves as the primary tool for resource allocation,
    balancing supply and demand while signalling shortages or surpluses.
    COMPETITION: This acts as a catalyst for efficiency and continuous economic development.
    ADVANTAGES: High innovation and responsiveness to consumer desires.
    DISADVANTAGES: Potential for inequality and the under-provision of public goods
    (like street lighting or national defence).

MIXED ECONOMY

Mixed economies combine features of both command and market systems.
The government regulates essential goods and services, influences production methods, and addresses social welfare issues.
The degree of intervention varies by country; for example, some nations lean toward a command structure (e.g., China),
while others lean toward a market structure (e.g., the USA). Economic freedom in these systems is typically measured by:

    -The rule of law (including property rights).
    -Government size and spending.
    -Regulatory efficiency.
    -Openness of markets.

THE PRODUCTION POSSIBILITY CURVE (PPC)

DEFINITION
The PPC is a graphical model representing the maximum combinations of
two goods an economy can produce given fixed resources and technology.
It illustrates scarcity, choice, and opportunity cost.

CLASSIFICATION OF GOODS
    - CONSUMER GOODS: Products intended for immediate use by individuals.
      Examples: Smartphones, clothing, food.
    - CAPITAL GOODS: Human-made tools used to create other goods.
      Examples: Industrial machinery, factory equipment, delivery trucks.

CORE ASSUMPTIONS
    - TWO-GOOD ECONOMY: Only two goods are produced (e.g., Capital vs Consumer).
    - FIXED RESOURCES: The quantity/quality of resources does not change.
    - FIXED TECHNOLOGY: The methods of production remain constant.
    - FULL EMPLOYMENT: All available resources are utilized.

ANALYSING POINTS ON THE PPC
    - ON THE CURVE: Represents PRODUCTIVE EFFICIENCY. All resources are
      used fully. Moving between points on the curve illustrates the
      TRADE-OFF (Opportunity Cost).
    - INSIDE THE CURVE: Represents INEFFICIENCY or unemployment.
      Resources are being wasted or under-utilized.
    - OUTSIDE THE CURVE: Represents UNATTAINABLE production levels with
      current resources/technology.

THE PRINCIPLE OF OPPORTUNITY COST
Because resources are scarce, increasing production of Capital Goods
requires a reduction in Consumer Goods.

 SHAPE: The curve is "Bowed-Out" (concave to the origin) because of
  INCREASING OPPORTUNITY COSTS. Resources are not equally suited
  to producing all types of goods.

ECONOMIC GROWTH
The entire PPC shifts OUTWARD if there is:
    - An increase in the quantity of resources.
    - An improvement in the quality of resources (e.g., better education).
    - Advances in technology.


CLASSIFICATION OF GOODS AND SERVICES

THE TWO CHARACTERISTICS OF GOODS
    To classify goods, economists look at two main features:
        - EXCLUDABILITY: Can people be prevented from using the good if they
          do not pay? (Yes = Excludable / No = Non-excludable).
        - RIVALRY: Does one person's use of the good reduce the amount
          available for others? (Yes = Rival / No = Non-rival).


PRIVATE GOODS (Pure Private)
    These are typical goods provided by the market system.
        - CHARACTERISTICS: Both EXCLUDABLE and RIVAL.
        - FEATURES: They are divisible into small units. If you buy it, you
          own it, and no one else can consume that specific unit.
        - EXAMPLES: Food, clothes, smartphones, personal hygiene products.

PUBLIC GOODS (Pure Public)
    These goods are contrary to private goods and are usually provided by
    the government because the free market fails to produce them.
        - CHARACTERISTICS: Both NON-EXCLUDABLE and NON-RIVAL.
        - FEATURES: Indivisible and consumed collectively. Adding one more
          user costs $0 and doesn't reduce the benefit to others.
        - THE FREE RIDER PROBLEM: Because you cannot exclude non-payers,
          people have no incentive to pay. This leads to "Market Failure."
        - EXAMPLES: National defense, street lighting, lighthouse signals.

QUASI-PUBLIC GOODS
    These goods do not meet all the criteria for "pure" goods. They
    fall into two sub-categories:

        NATURAL MONOPOLY GOODS (Club Goods)
        - CHARACTERISTICS: EXCLUDABLE but NON-RIVAL.
        - EXAMPLES: Netflix/Streaming services, Internet, Private Gyms.
          (You must pay to enter, but your use doesn't stop others from using).

        COMMON RESOURCES
        - CHARACTERISTICS: NON-EXCLUDABLE but RIVAL.
        - THE PROBLEM: Leads to "The Tragedy of the Commons" (Overconsumption).
        - EXAMPLES: Fish in the ocean, public forests, clean air.

THE IMPACT OF CONGESTION
    Some public goods can lose their "non-rival" status as they get
    crowded. This is the "quantitative dimension."
        - EXAMPLE: A public highway or a beach.
        - ANALYSIS: When empty, they are non-rival. When crowded (congested),
          one person's presence reduces the utility/space for others.
