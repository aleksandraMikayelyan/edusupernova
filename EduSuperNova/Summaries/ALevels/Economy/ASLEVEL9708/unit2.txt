THE PRICE SYSTEM AND THE MICROECONOMY

DEMAND AND SUPPLY CURVES

    THE NATURE OF DEMAND

        Demand is the quantity of a good or service that consumers are both
        WILLING and ABLE to buy at a given price in a given time period.
        * EFFECTIVE DEMAND: Only demand backed by the ability to pay (money)
          is recognized by the market.

    THE LAW OF DEMAND
        There is an INVERSE (negative) relationship between Price (P) and
        Quantity Demanded , ceteris paribus.
        - When Price rises, Quantity Demanded falls.
        - When Price falls, Quantity Demanded rises.
        - RESULT: The Demand curve slopes downwards from left to right.

    WHY DOES THE DEMAND CURVE SLOPE DOWNWARDS?
        - INCOME EFFECT: As price falls, a consumer's "real income" (purchasing
          power) increases, allowing them to buy more.
        - SUBSTITUTION EFFECT: As price falls, the good becomes cheaper
          relative to its substitutes, so consumers switch toward it.
        - LAW OF DIMINISHING MARGINAL UTILITY: As a person consumes more units,
          the extra satisfaction (utility) declines, so they are only willing
          to buy more at a lower price.

    MOVEMENTS VS. SHIFTS
        * MOVEMENT ALONG THE CURVE:
          - Caused ONLY by a change in the PRICE of the good itself.
          - An upward movement is a "Contraction" in demand.
          - A downward movement is an "Expansion" in demand.

        * SHIFT OF THE CURVE:
          - Caused by NON-PRICE DETERMINANTS (e.g., Income, Tastes, Sub-prices).
          - A shift to the RIGHT is an "Increase" in demand.
          - A shift to the LEFT is a "Decrease" in demand.

    CETERIS PARIBUS ASSUMPTION
        This is a Latin phrase meaning "all other things being equal." We use
        this to isolate the effect of one variable (like price) while
        assuming other factors (like income) do not change.

    THE SUPPLY CURVE

    THE NATURE OF SUPPLY

    Supply is the quantity of a good or service that producers are WILLING
    and ABLE to provide to the market at a given price in a given time period.
    * PROFIT MOTIVE: Producers usually only supply more if the price covers
      the extra cost of production.

    THE LAW OF SUPPLY
    There is a DIRECT (positive) relationship between Price (P) and
    Quantity Supplied, ceteris paribus.
        - When Price rises, Quantity Supplied increases.
        - When Price falls, Quantity Supplied decreases.
        - RESULT: The Supply curve slopes UPWARDS from left to right.

    WHY DOES THE SUPPLY CURVE SLOPE UPWARDS?
    * PROFIT INCENTIVE: Higher prices mean higher potential profit,
      encouraging firms to increase output.
    * DIMINISHING MARGINAL RETURNS: As production increases, the cost of
      producing an extra unit (marginal cost) often rises. Therefore, firms
      need a higher price to cover these higher costs.
    * NEW ENTRANTS: Higher prices attract new firms into the industry.

    MOVEMENTS VS. SHIFTS
        * MOVEMENT ALONG THE CURVE:
          - Caused ONLY by a change in the PRICE of the good itself.
          - An upward movement is an "Extension" in supply.
          - A downward movement is a "Contraction" in supply.

        * SHIFT OF THE CURVE:
          - Caused by NON-PRICE DETERMINANTS (Factors affecting costs).
          - A shift to the RIGHT (S1 to S2) is an "Increase" in supply.
          - A shift to the LEFT (S1 to S3) is a "Decrease" in supply.

        NON-PRICE DETERMINANTS OF SUPPLY (The "PINTS" Acronym)
        - P: PRODUCTION COSTS (Wages, raw materials, energy, rent).
        - I: INDIRECT TAXES (Government taxes on spending, e.g., VAT).
        - N: NUMBER OF FIRMS (Market size/Competition).
        - T: TECHNOLOGY (Improvements increase efficiency and lower costs).
        - S: SUBSIDIES (Government grants that lower production costs).
        - O: OTHER (Weather in agriculture, future price expectations).

        JOINT AND COMPETITIVE SUPPLY
        - JOINT SUPPLY: When the production of one good automatically
          produces another (e.g., Beef and Leather).
        - COMPETITIVE SUPPLY: When a resource can be used for two different
          goods (e.g., A farmer using land for wheat OR barley).

        ELASTICITY OF DEMAND

        PRICE ELASTICITY OF DEMAND (PED)
        Definition: Measures the responsiveness of Quantity Demanded (Qd) to
        a change in the Price (P) of the good itself.

            FORMULA:
            PED = (% Change in Qd) / (% Change in Price)

        * THE MIDPOINT METHOD (Standard for precision):
          % Change = (Change / Average) * 100
          Average = (Initial + Final) / 2


        INTERPRETING PED VALUES
            (Note: PED is usually negative due to the Law of Demand, but we often
            use the absolute value).
        - PED = 0: PERFECTLY INELASTIC (Vertical line). No change in Qd
          regardless of price (e.g., life-saving insulin).
        - 0 < PED < 1: INELASTIC (Steep curve). Qd changes by a smaller %
          than Price (e.g., fuel, salt).
        - PED = 1: UNIT ELASTIC. % change in Qd = % change in Price.
        - PED > 1: ELASTIC (Flat curve). Qd changes by a larger % than Price
          (e.g., luxury cars, specific brands).
        - PED = Infinity: PERFECTLY ELASTIC (Horizontal line). Even a tiny
          price change leads to an infinite change in Qd.

        DETERMINANTS OF PED (Why is it high or low?)

        - SUBSTITUTES: More substitutes = More Elastic.
        - NECESSITY VS. LUXURY: Necessities are Inelastic; Luxuries are Elastic.
        - DEFINITION: Broad categories (Food) are Inelastic; narrow brands
          (Ben & Jerry's Ice Cream) are Elastic.
        - % OF INCOME: Cheap items (Matches/Salt) are Inelastic; expensive
          items (Houses/Cars) are Elastic.
        - TIME: Demand is more Elastic in the LONG RUN as consumers find
          alternatives.

        INCOME ELASTICITY OF DEMAND (YED)
        Definition: Measures how Qd changes when consumer INCOME (Y) changes.

            FORMULA:
            YED = (% Change in Qd) / (% Change in Income)

        THE SIGN MATTERS:
        - POSITIVE (+) YED: NORMAL GOOD. (Income up = Demand up).
          * 0 to 1: Necessity (Inelastic).
          * > 1: Luxury (Elastic).
        - NEGATIVE (-) YED: INFERIOR GOOD. (Income up = Demand falls,
          e.g., second-hand clothes, instant noodles).

        CROSS-PRICE ELASTICITY OF DEMAND (XED)
        Definition: Measures how Qd of Good A reacts to a price change of Good B.

            FORMULA:
            XED = (% Change in Qd of Good A) / (% Change in Price of Good B)

        THE SIGN MATTERS:
        - POSITIVE (+) XED: SUBSTITUTES (e.g., Coke and Pepsi). If price of
          one rises, demand for the other rises.
        - NEGATIVE (-) XED: COMPLEMENTS (e.g., Printers and Ink). If price of
          one rises, demand for the other falls.
        - ZERO (0) XED: UNRELATED GOODS.

        PRICE ELASTICITY OF SUPPLY (PES)

        DEFINITION
        PES measures the responsiveness of the Quantity Supplied (Qs) to a
        change in the Price (P) of the good itself.

            FORMULA:
            PES = (% Change in Qs) / (% Change in Price)

        * Note: Unlike Demand, PES is ALWAYS POSITIVE because of the direct
          relationship between Price and Supply.

        INTERPRETING PES VALUES

        - PES = 0: PERFECTLY INELASTIC (Vertical line). Supply cannot change
          regardless of price (e.g., stadium seats for a specific match).
        - 0 < PES < 1: INELASTIC (Steep curve). Qs is not very responsive
          to price (e.g., complex manufactured goods, agricultural crops).
        - PES = 1: UNIT ELASTIC. % change in Qs = % change in Price.
        - PES > 1: ELASTIC (Flat curve). Qs is very responsive to price.
        - PES = Infinity: PERFECTLY ELASTIC (Horizontal line). Producers
          supply any amount at a specific price.

        DETERMINANTS OF PES (What makes supply flexible?)
        The "BRITS" Acronym is helpful here:

            - B: BARRIERS TO ENTRY. High barriers (patents, high start-up costs)
              make supply INELASTIC.
            - R: RAW MATERIALS. If resources are hard to find, supply is INELASTIC.
            - I: INVENTORY (Stocks). If a firm can store its goods easily (like
              canned food), supply is ELASTIC. Perishable goods (fresh fish) are
              INELASTIC.
            - T: TIME PERIOD.
                  * Momentary Period: Supply is fixed/Perfectly Inelastic.
                  * Short Run: Inelastic (only some factors like labor can change).
                  * Long Run: Elastic (all factors including factory size can change).
            - S: SPARE CAPACITY. If a factory is running at 50% capacity, it can
              quickly increase output if price rises (ELASTIC). If it is at
              Full Capacity, it is INELASTIC.

        THE TIME FACTOR IN DEPTH
            - MOMENTARY RUN: All factors are fixed. Supply cannot react.
            - SHORT RUN (SR): At least one factor of production is fixed (e.g.,
              size of the factory). Supply can increase by adding more labor.
            - LONG RUN (LR): All factors are variable. Firms can build new
              factories or leave the industry. Supply is highly elastic.

        INTERACTION OF SUPPLY AND DEMAND

        MARKET EQUILIBRIUM

        Equilibrium occurs at the intersection of the Demand and Supply
        curves (Point E).
        - EQUILIBRIUM PRICE (Pe): The price where Qd = Qs. The market "clears."
        - EQUILIBRIUM QUANTITY (Qe): The amount bought/sold at the Pe.
        - TOTAL REVENUE (TR): Price x Quantity (P x Q).

        2. MARKET DISEQUILIBRIUM
        - EXCESS DEMAND (Shortage): Occurs when Price < Pe.
          * Effect: Qd > Qs.
          * Market Reaction: Buyers outbid each other, forcing prices UP.
        - EXCESS SUPPLY (Surplus): Occurs when Price > Pe.
          * Effect: Qs > Qd.
          * Market Reaction: Unsold stock forces producers to lower prices DOWN.

        FUNCTIONS OF THE PRICE MECHANISM (The "SIR" Acronym)
            When demand or supply shifts, the price changes to fix the market:
            - SIGNALLING: Price changes provide information to buyers and sellers
              about changing market conditions.
            - INCENTIVE: Higher prices encourage producers to supply more (profit)
              and consumers to buy less.
            - RATIONING: When resources are scarce, the price rises so that only
              those who value the good most (and can afford it) receive it.

        THE EFFECTS OF SHIFTS (The "Four Laws")

        - Demand Increases (D shifts Right) -> Price Increases, Quantity Increases.
        - Demand Decreases (D shifts Left)  -> Price Decreases, Quantity Decreases.
        - Supply Increases (S shifts Right) -> Price Decreases, Quantity Increases.
        - Supply Decreases (S shifts Left)  -> Price Increases, Quantity Decreases.

        SIMULTANEOUS SHIFTS (Double Shifts)

            When both curves move at once, the outcome for one variable (P or Q)
            is often "Indeterminate" (uncertain) unless we know which shift
            is larger.

                * BOTH INCREASE (D up, S up):
                  - Quantity will DEFINITELY Increase.
                  - Price is Indeterminate (depends on which shift is bigger).
                * BOTH DECREASE (D down, S down):
                  - Quantity will DEFINITELY Decrease.
                  - Price is Indeterminate.
                * OPPOSITE DIRECTIONS (e.g., D up, S down):
                  - Price will DEFINITELY Increase.
                  - Quantity is Indeterminate.

        CONSUMER AND PRODUCER SURPLUS

        CONSUMER SURPLUS (CS)
        Definition: The difference between the maximum price a consumer is
        willing and able to pay for a good and the actual market price paid.

            * THE MARGINAL BENEFIT: The Demand curve represents the Marginal
              Benefit (MB) to the consumer. As we consume more, our willingness
              to pay for an extra unit decreases.
            * ON THE GRAPH: CS is the area BELOW the Demand curve and ABOVE
              the Market Price.

            * CHANGES IN CS:
              - PRICE FALLS: CS increases (Consumers pay less for what they were
                already buying, plus new consumers enter the market).
              - PRICE RISES: CS decreases (Consumers pay more, and some leave
                the market).

        PRODUCER SURPLUS (PS)
        Definition: The difference between the minimum price a producer is
        willing to accept for a good and the actual market price received.

            * THE MARGINAL COST: The Supply curve represents the Marginal
              Cost (MC) of production. A firm will only sell a unit if the
              price is at least equal to the cost of making it.
            * ON THE GRAPH: PS is the area ABOVE the Supply curve and BELOW
              the Market Price.

            CHANGES IN PS:
              - PRICE RISES: PS increases (Producers get a higher price for
                existing units, plus new producers enter the market).
              - PRICE FALLS: PS decreases (Profit margins shrink and some
                high-cost producers exit).

        SOCIAL (TOTAL) SURPLUS
            Total Surplus = Consumer Surplus + Producer Surplus.
            - This represents the TOTAL WELFARE or benefit to society from
              the production and consumption of a good.
            - In a free market with no externalities, Total Surplus is
              MAXIMIZED at the Equilibrium point (Allocative Efficiency).

        AS LEVEL EXAM TIP: THE "TRIANGLE" CALCULATION

            You may be asked to calculate the value of CS or PS using the
            formula for the area of a triangle:
            Area = 1/2 x Base x Height
            - Base: The equilibrium quantity.
            - Height: The difference between the intercept on the Y-axis
              and the market price.
