STYLE

STYLE ON THE ACT: Style questions ask you to evaluate the effectiveness of word choices, sentence structures, and phrasing — not just whether something is grammatically correct, but whether it's the best choice for the passage's purpose, audience, and tone.

CONCISENESS AND PRECISION:

The ACT's most reliable style principle: shorter is better, as long as meaning is preserved.

When two options are both grammatically correct, the more concise one is almost always right. Ask yourself: does this longer version add any new meaning, or just more words?

REDUNDANCY: Watch for words and phrases that say the same thing twice.
- "He was a true and genuine leader" → "He was a genuine leader"
- "She whispered quietly" → "She whispered" (whispering is already quiet)
- "They collaborated together" → "They collaborated"
- "The biography of her life" → "Her biography"
- "unexpected surprise" → "surprise"

VAGUENESS vs. SPECIFICITY:

Vague language weakens writing. Specific language strengthens it.
- Weak: "The results were good." / Strong: "The results exceeded expectations."
- Weak: "Many people think..." / Strong: "Researchers have found..."

When a question asks which option best achieves a specific purpose (e.g., "conveys the researcher's excitement"), choose the option with the most vivid and precise language.

TONE CONSISTENCY: Each ACT passage has a specific voice and tone. Maintain that tone when selecting wording.

A formal academic essay should not suddenly use:
- Slang: "The experiment was super cool."
- Colloquialisms: "The results were kind of surprising."
- Overly casual address: "You can see that the data shows..."

A personal narrative should not suddenly shift to:
- Overly technical jargon without explanation
- Cold, detached scientific language

IMPORTANT: When evaluating style, read several sentences around the underlined portion to get a feel for the passage's established tone.

SENTENCE VARIETY AND RHYTHM:

Well-crafted writing varies sentence length and structure. Short sentences create punch and emphasis. Longer sentences can develop complex ideas and show relationships between them.

The ACT may ask you to choose a revision that improves sentence variety. Watch for:
- A series of short, choppy sentences that could be combined
- A single extremely long sentence that could be broken up or restructured
- Monotonous sentence openings (all starting with "The" or all starting with a subject)

COMBINING SENTENCES: When asked to combine two sentences, the best option:
- Preserves all essential information
- Eliminates unnecessary words
- Uses the most logical connective word to show the relationship
- Results in a smooth, natural-sounding sentence

FIGURATIVE LANGUAGE: Similes, metaphors, and other figurative devices can strengthen writing — but only if they fit naturally. Watch for:
- Mixed metaphors (combining two incompatible images): "We need to iron out the bottlenecks." (ironing and bottlenecks don't mix)
- Clichés that weaken rather than strengthen the writing
- Figurative language that clashes with a factual or scientific tone

PARALLELISM IN STYLE: Beyond grammar, parallel structure creates rhythmic flow and clarity.
- "The project required creativity, dedication, and [to work hard]" → should be "hard work"
- Lists and paired ideas should feel balanced and symmetrical

ACTIVE AND PASSIVE VOICE:

Active voice is almost always preferable in ACT style.
- Passive: "Mistakes were made." (vague, weak)
- Active: "The team made mistakes." (clear, direct)

Passive voice is acceptable when the doer is unknown or irrelevant: "The artifact was discovered in 1923."

EMPHASIS AND FOCUS:

The part of a sentence that comes at the end receives the most emphasis. Good writers strategically place important information at the end.
- "Despite the setbacks, she won." (emphasis on winning)
- "She won, despite the setbacks." (emphasis on setbacks)

When an ACT question asks which revision "best emphasizes" something, look for the option that places that information in the most prominent position.

AVOIDING AWKWARD PHRASING:

Trust your ear. If something sounds awkward when you read it aloud (even mentally), it's probably wrong. Common awkward constructions:
- Split infinitives that create awkwardness (though not always wrong): "to quickly and carefully analyze"
- Unnecessarily complex noun phrases: "the analysis of the results by the researchers" → "the researchers' analysis of the results"
- Awkward passive constructions: "It was decided by the committee" → "The committee decided"

REMEMBER: Style questions don't have a simple rule to apply. You must read in context, consider the passage's purpose and tone, and ask what makes the writing clearest, most precise, and most natural-sounding.
