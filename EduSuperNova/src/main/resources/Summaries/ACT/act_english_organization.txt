ORGANIZATION

PARAGRAPH AND ESSAY STRUCTURE: Organization questions ask you to evaluate where sentences belong, in what order information should be presented, and whether the structure of the passage is logical and effective.

THE THREE LEVELS OF ORGANIZATION:
1. Sentence level — word order within a sentence
2. Paragraph level — sentence order within a paragraph
3. Essay level — paragraph order within the whole passage

SENTENCE PLACEMENT QUESTIONS:

When asked where to place a new sentence or move an existing one, follow this process:
1. Read the sentence to understand its content and purpose
2. Look for clues: transition words, pronouns (it, they, this, these), logical connections
3. Check each proposed location — does the sentence connect naturally to what comes before AND after?
4. The sentence must fit the surrounding context both logically and grammatically

KEY CONCEPT: Pronouns as clues — if a sentence uses "it" or "they" without a clear antecedent, it can't come first. Find the sentence that introduces the noun the pronoun refers to.

ORDERING SENTENCES IN A PARAGRAPH:

Chronological order — for narratives and processes, events should follow time order. Watch for time signal words: first, then, next, later, afterward, finally, before, after, during.

Logical order — for arguments and explanations, start with a claim or topic, then provide supporting evidence or details. Don't put examples before the point they illustrate.

Order of importance — can go from least to most important (for persuasive build-up) or most to least (for emphasis).

PARAGRAPH PLACEMENT QUESTIONS:

When asked where a paragraph fits in the essay:
- Read the first and last sentences of each paragraph to map the flow
- Identify the essay's overall structure (chronological? thematic? problem-solution?)
- The new paragraph should connect naturally to what comes before and after
- Look for transition words at the start of the paragraph — they signal the relationship to the preceding paragraph

TRANSITIONS: Transitions connect ideas within and between sentences and paragraphs. The ACT tests whether you can identify the correct relationship between ideas and choose the appropriate transition.

ADDITION: furthermore, moreover, in addition, also, besides, additionally
CONTRAST: however, nevertheless, on the other hand, yet, although, despite, in contrast, conversely, while, whereas
CAUSE AND EFFECT: therefore, consequently, as a result, thus, hence, because, since, so
SEQUENCE/TIME: first, then, next, finally, afterward, previously, simultaneously, subsequently
EXAMPLE: for example, for instance, specifically, in particular, to illustrate
EMPHASIS: indeed, in fact, certainly, above all, most importantly
CONCESSION: admittedly, granted, it is true that, even though

IMPORTANT: Wrong transition words are a frequent ACT trap. Read both the sentence before AND the sentence after the transition to determine the actual logical relationship.

Test each transition: "however" signals contrast — are the ideas actually contrasting? "Therefore" signals conclusion — is the second idea actually a consequence of the first?

OPENING AND CLOSING SENTENCES:

A strong opening:
- Introduces the topic clearly
- Engages the reader
- Does NOT begin with an irrelevant detail

A strong conclusion:
- Wraps up the main idea
- May offer a broader implication or call to action
- Does NOT introduce completely new information
- Does NOT abruptly end or trail off without closure

RELEVANCE: The ACT asks whether a sentence or detail should be included in a paragraph.

Ask: Does this sentence relate to the main topic of the paragraph? Does it advance the paragraph's purpose? If a detail is interesting but off-topic, it should be deleted.

If asked whether to add a sentence, evaluate: Does it serve the paragraph's purpose? Does it fit the tone? Is it specific enough to add value?

UNITY: Each paragraph should have one clear main idea. Every sentence should support that idea directly.

COHERENCE: Ideas within and between paragraphs should flow logically. One idea should lead naturally to the next.

REMEMBER: Always read the full paragraph (and sometimes the full essay) before answering organization questions. Understanding the overall purpose is essential to placing sentences and paragraphs correctly.
