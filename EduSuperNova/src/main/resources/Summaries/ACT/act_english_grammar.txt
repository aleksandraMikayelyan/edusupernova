GRAMMAR AND USAGE

SUBJECT-VERB AGREEMENT: The verb must agree in number with its subject, not with words that come between them.

The key rule is to identify the true subject and ignore prepositional phrases, clauses, or parenthetical elements that separate the subject from the verb.

Common traps:
- Phrases beginning with "along with," "as well as," "in addition to," "together with" do NOT make a singular subject plural
- Inverted sentences (verb before subject): "There are many reasons" — "reasons" is the subject
- Collective nouns (team, group, jury, family) take singular verbs when acting as one unit

EXAMPLES OF SUBJECT-VERB TRAPS:
- "The box of chocolates [are/is] on the table" → IS (subject is "box," not "chocolates")
- "Neither the players nor the coach [was/were] ready" → WAS (verb agrees with the closer noun "coach")
- "The jury [have/has] reached a verdict" → HAS (jury acting as one unit)

KEY CONCEPT: Indefinite pronouns as subjects
- Always singular: each, every, either, neither, one, anyone, anyone, everyone, someone, no one, nobody, everybody, somebody, anything, everything, something, nothing
- Always plural: both, few, many, several, others
- Either singular or plural depending on context: all, any, most, none, some (look at what follows "of")

PRONOUN-ANTECEDENT AGREEMENT: A pronoun must agree with its antecedent in number, gender, and person.

- Singular antecedents require singular pronouns
- Collective nouns generally take singular pronouns
- Indefinite pronoun antecedents follow the same rules as subject-verb agreement

IMPORTANT: "Their" is NOT acceptable for a singular antecedent in formal ACT writing. Use "his or her" for indefinite pronoun antecedents, or rewrite the sentence to use a plural noun.

PRONOUN CASE: Pronouns change form depending on their grammatical role.

SUBJECT PRONOUNS: I, he, she, we, they, who
OBJECT PRONOUNS: me, him, her, us, them, whom

The trick for compound constructions: Remove the other person and test the pronoun alone.
- "She gave the award to John and [I/me]" → Remove "John and" → "She gave the award to me" → ME is correct

IMPORTANT: After prepositions, always use object pronouns.
- "between you and me" (NEVER "between you and I")
- "for him and her"
- "with us students"

WHO vs. WHOM:
- WHO = subject (does the action): "Who called?" → He called → WHO
- WHOM = object (receives the action): "Whom did you call?" → You called him → WHOM

VERB TENSES: Verbs must be consistent in tense throughout a passage unless a shift in time is clearly indicated.

SIMPLE TENSES: past, present, future
PERFECT TENSES: past perfect (had + past participle), present perfect (have/has + past participle), future perfect (will have + past participle)

IMPORTANT: Past perfect shows that one past action happened BEFORE another past action.
- "By the time she arrived, he had already left."

Use present tense when discussing literature, film, or art: "In the novel, the character struggles with identity." (not "struggled")

MODIFIER PLACEMENT: Modifiers should be placed as close as possible to what they modify.

MISPLACED MODIFIER: A modifier placed too far from the word it modifies.
- Incorrect: "Running down the street, the rain soaked her dress."
- Correct: "Running down the street, she was soaked by the rain."

DANGLING MODIFIER: A modifier that has nothing to logically modify in the sentence.
- Incorrect: "After studying all night, the test seemed easy."
- Correct: "After studying all night, she found the test easy."

KEY CONCEPT: The subject of a modifying participial phrase must be the subject of the main clause.

PARALLEL STRUCTURE: Items in a series or paired elements must be in the same grammatical form.

In lists: "She likes hiking, swimming, and to run" → WRONG
Corrected: "She likes hiking, swimming, and running" (all gerunds)

With correlative conjunctions (not only...but also, either...or, neither...nor, both...and):
- "Not only did she sing, but she also danced" ✓
- "Not only singing but also to dance" ✗

COMPARISONS: Always complete comparisons clearly and correctly.
- Incorrect: "Her score is higher than any student." (compares score to student — illogical)
- Correct: "Her score is higher than that of any other student."

ADJECTIVES vs. ADVERBS:
- Adjectives modify nouns and pronouns
- Adverbs modify verbs, adjectives, and other adverbs (often end in -ly)
- After linking verbs (feel, look, seem, appear, taste, smell), use adjectives: "The soup tastes good" (not "well")
- "Well" is an adjective when referring to health: "She doesn't feel well."

DOUBLE NEGATIVES: ACT English follows standard written English rules — avoid double negatives.
- "I don't have no money" → "I don't have any money" or "I have no money"
- Barely, hardly, scarcely function as negatives — don't pair with "not"

COMMONLY CONFUSED WORDS:
- AFFECT (verb) vs. EFFECT (noun): "The cold affects my mood." / "The effect was significant."
- LAY (to place something) vs. LIE (to recline): "Lay the book down." / "Lie down and rest."
- FEWER vs. LESS: Fewer = countable items; Less = uncountable quantities
- AMONG vs. BETWEEN: Among = 3+ items; Between = 2 items (though "between" can apply to multiple distinct entities)
- THAT vs. WHICH: That introduces restrictive clauses (no comma); Which introduces non-restrictive clauses (with comma)
