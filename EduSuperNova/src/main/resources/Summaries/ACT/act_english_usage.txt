USAGE AND STYLE

WORD CHOICE: The ACT tests whether you can identify the most precise and appropriate word for a given context.

CONCISENESS: The ACT consistently rewards shorter, clearer phrasing. If two answers are grammatically correct, choose the more concise one.

Redundant expressions to eliminate:
- "end result" → "result"
- "past history" → "history"
- "future plans" → "plans"
- "completely finished" → "finished"
- "basic fundamentals" → "fundamentals"
- "return back" → "return"
- "close proximity" → "proximity" or "nearby"
- "each and every" → "each" or "every"
- "first and foremost" → "first"
- "the reason is because" → "the reason is that" or "because"

KEY CONCEPT: If a word or phrase can be deleted without changing the meaning, the ACT wants it deleted.

IDIOMS: Some ACT questions test whether you know the conventional preposition that follows certain words.

Common idiomatic prepositions:
- Interested IN (not "interested about")
- Agree WITH a person; agree ON a topic
- Differ FROM; different FROM
- Compared TO (show similarity); compared WITH (examine differences)
- Responsible FOR
- Capable OF
- Concerned ABOUT; concerned WITH
- Composed OF
- Based ON
- Fascinated BY / WITH
- Native TO (a place)
- Independent OF
- Participate IN
- Result IN (produces an outcome); result FROM (caused by)

COMMONLY CONFUSED IDIOMS:
- "Try to" (attempt): "Try to finish by noon." (NOT "try and finish")
- "Superior to" (NOT "superior than")
- "Preferable to" (NOT "preferable than")
- "Center on" (NOT "center around")

WORD PAIRS AND DISTINCTIONS:

AMOUNT vs. NUMBER: Amount is for uncountable things; Number is for countable things.
- "a large amount of water" / "a large number of bottles"

SINCE vs. BECAUSE: "Since" properly indicates time; "because" indicates causation. On formal tests, use "because" for cause-and-effect.

AS vs. LIKE:
- LIKE introduces nouns and pronouns: "She sings like a professional."
- AS introduces clauses (with a verb): "She sings as a professional does."

THAT vs. WHO:
- WHO refers to people
- THAT refers to things (and sometimes animals)
- "The student who studied..." (not "that studied")

WHICH vs. THAT (restrictive vs. non-restrictive):
- THAT: restrictive (essential) — no comma: "The car that she bought is red."
- WHICH: non-restrictive (extra info) — with comma: "Her car, which she bought last year, is red."

ACTIVE vs. PASSIVE VOICE: The ACT prefers active voice for clarity and conciseness.
- Passive: "The experiment was conducted by the scientists."
- Active: "The scientists conducted the experiment."

Use passive voice only when the doer of the action is unknown, unimportant, or deliberately omitted.

TONE AND REGISTER: Match the tone of your word choices to the context of the passage. The ACT passages have a consistent tone — formal essays should not suddenly use slang, and conversational pieces should not use unnecessarily stiff, formal diction.

Watch for:
- Informal/colloquial words in formal contexts ("kids" vs. "children," "a lot" vs. "many")
- Overly formal or pretentious words where simpler ones would work better
- Clichés and vague language where precise language is needed

WORDINESS: Certain phrases are always verbose and can be replaced:
- "the fact that" → often deleted or replaced by "that" or "because"
- "in order to" → usually just "to"
- "due to the fact that" → "because"
- "at this point in time" → "now"
- "in the event that" → "if"
- "with regard to" → "regarding" or "about"
- "for the purpose of" → "to"

DICTION PRECISION: Some questions ask you to choose the word that most precisely fits the meaning.

EXAMPLE: A paragraph about scientific discovery would call for "hypothesize" rather than "guess," "investigate" rather than "look at," "demonstrated" rather than "showed."

IMPORTANT: Don't choose a word just because it sounds more impressive. Choose the word that most accurately and naturally captures the intended meaning.

SENTENCE VARIETY: Good writing mixes sentence lengths and structures. While the ACT doesn't often directly test this, recognizing awkward or choppy constructions is part of revising for style.

DOUBLE NEGATIVES IN USAGE:
- "I can't hardly see" → "I can hardly see" or "I can't see"
- "without no" → "without any"
- "not... nothing" → "not... anything" or "nothing"

REMEMBER: When comparing answer choices, always ask: (1) Is it grammatically correct? (2) Is it clear? (3) Is it concise? The correct answer is the one that satisfies all three criteria.
