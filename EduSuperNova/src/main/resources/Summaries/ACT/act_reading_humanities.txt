READING — HUMANITIES PASSAGES

OVERVIEW: The Humanities passage on the ACT Reading test covers topics from art, music, film, architecture, philosophy, cultural criticism, literary history, and memoir. These passages often combine personal perspective with informed analysis, making them distinctive from both fiction and purely academic writing.

CHARACTERISTICS OF HUMANITIES PASSAGES:

Humanities passages frequently:
- Are written in first person (memoir, personal essay, cultural criticism)
- Blend personal experience with broader cultural or intellectual observation
- Use more figurative and evaluative language than science passages
- Present a strong authorial voice with opinions and aesthetic judgments
- Discuss a creative work (a novel, painting, symphony) or a cultural movement

KEY CONCEPT: Humanities passages are often more "voice-driven" than other ACT passages. Identifying the author's perspective, enthusiasm, or critique is central to understanding these texts.

THE AUTHOR'S PERSPECTIVE:

Unlike natural science passages that aim for objectivity, humanities passages often explicitly share the author's viewpoint. Be attuned to:
- Admiration or enthusiasm for a subject
- Critical distance or skepticism
- Personal connection or nostalgia
- Intellectual curiosity or wonder

Look for evaluative language: "groundbreaking," "derivative," "strikingly original," "surprisingly neglected," "deeply flawed."

MEMOIR AND PERSONAL ESSAY:

When the humanities passage is memoir or personal essay:
- The "I" narrator is also the author (unlike fiction, where the narrator is a character)
- Personal anecdotes serve to illustrate larger ideas or themes
- The author's emotional response to events is significant
- Look for reflection and interpretation, not just narration of events

CRITICAL ESSAY ON ART OR CULTURE:

When the humanities passage analyzes a cultural work or phenomenon:
- Identify the work being discussed and the author's central claim about it
- Note how the author uses description, historical context, and comparison to support the argument
- Be aware that the author's evaluative terms (good, important, significant, revolutionary) are claims that need evidence

QUESTION TYPES FOR HUMANITIES:

AUTHOR'S PURPOSE: Why is the author writing this? To appreciate, critique, historicize, or personally reflect on a subject?

TONE AND ATTITUDE: Humanities passages often have nuanced tones — not simply positive or negative. Learn tone vocabulary:
- Reverent: deep respect or admiration
- Nostalgic: wistful longing for the past
- Ambivalent: having mixed feelings
- Ironic: saying the opposite of what is meant
- Celebratory: enthusiastic praise
- Elegiac: mournful or lamenting (like an elegy)
- Critical: identifying flaws or problems
- Measured: carefully balanced, neither extreme

FUNCTION OF ANECDOTE: When a personal story appears in a humanities passage, it is not there merely to entertain. Ask: what larger point does this anecdote illustrate?

VOCABULARY IN CONTEXT: Humanities passages use rich, sometimes unusual vocabulary. The strategy remains the same: substitute answer choices and check for contextual fit.

STRUCTURE OF HUMANITIES PASSAGES:

Humanities passages often follow one of these structures:
- Personal reflection → broader cultural insight
- Description of a work → analysis of its significance
- Historical context → evaluation of legacy
- Comparison between two works or artists
- Challenge to a received view: "It is commonly thought that X, but in fact..."

IMPORTANT: The movement from specific to general (or general to specific) is very common in humanities essays. Understand how the opening and closing paragraphs relate to each other.

CONNECTING DETAILS TO MAIN IDEAS:

Every specific detail in a humanities passage — a quote, a specific painting described, a date — exists to support the author's larger argument. When asked why a detail is included, ask: what claim does it support?

EVALUATING THE AUTHOR'S ARGUMENT:

Some questions ask you to assess the author's reasoning:
- Does the evidence actually support the conclusion?
- Are there alternative interpretations of the evidence?
- What assumptions does the author make?

NOTE: Don't import your own opinions about the subject. Evaluate the argument on the basis of what the passage says.

PAIRED HUMANITIES PASSAGES:

If paired, humanities passages often represent:
- Two critics with different evaluations of the same work
- Two different eras commenting on the same artistic tradition
- One passage giving historical background and another giving personal response

KEY STRATEGY: Identify the core difference in perspective before answering comparison questions.

READING EFFICIENTLY:

Humanities passages reward close reading of the opening paragraph (establishes topic and author's stance) and the closing paragraph (reveals the author's final conclusion or broader significance). Skim the middle for structure and return for details when needed.

REMEMBER: In humanities passages, vague answers like "to provide background information" are usually wrong — they don't capture the specific purpose. The best answers are specific: "to illustrate how the artist's early struggles influenced her later style."
