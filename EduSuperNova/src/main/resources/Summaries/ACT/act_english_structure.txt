SENTENCE STRUCTURE

CLAUSES AND PHRASES: Understanding the basic units of sentences is essential for identifying and correcting structural errors.

INDEPENDENT CLAUSE: A complete thought with a subject and a verb. Can stand alone as a sentence.
- "The scientist published her findings."

DEPENDENT CLAUSE: Has a subject and verb but cannot stand alone. Introduced by subordinating conjunctions (because, although, since, when, if, while, unless, until, after, before, as, that, which, who) or relative pronouns.
- "Because the results were unexpected..."
- "Although she had studied..."

PHRASE: A group of words without a subject-verb pair. Cannot stand alone.
- Prepositional phrase: "in the morning," "under the table"
- Participial phrase: "Running down the street," "Exhausted by the effort"
- Infinitive phrase: "to complete the assignment"
- Gerund phrase: "Swimming in the ocean"

SENTENCE TYPES:

SIMPLE: One independent clause
- "She runs every morning."

COMPOUND: Two or more independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction, semicolon, or colon
- "She runs every morning, and she swims in the afternoon."

COMPLEX: One independent clause + one or more dependent clauses
- "Although she was tired, she finished the race."

COMPOUND-COMPLEX: Two or more independent clauses + at least one dependent clause
- "She was tired, but she finished the race because she had trained for months."

SENTENCE FRAGMENTS: A fragment is an incomplete sentence — it lacks a subject, lacks a verb, or is a dependent clause standing alone.

Fragment types:
- Missing subject: "Ran across the field." → Who ran?
- Missing verb: "The tall building on the corner." → What about it?
- Dependent clause alone: "Although the weather was perfect." → This needs an independent clause
- Participial phrase alone: "Running along the shore." → Needs a main clause

KEY CONCEPT: Fragments can be fixed by: adding the missing element, connecting to an adjacent sentence, or restructuring.

RUN-ON SENTENCES: Two independent clauses incorrectly joined.

FUSED SENTENCE: No punctuation between clauses
- "She studied all night she aced the exam." → WRONG

COMMA SPLICE: Only a comma between clauses
- "She studied all night, she aced the exam." → WRONG

Fixes:
1. Period: "She studied all night. She aced the exam."
2. Semicolon: "She studied all night; she aced the exam."
3. Comma + conjunction: "She studied all night, and she aced the exam."
4. Subordination: "Because she studied all night, she aced the exam."

SUBORDINATION AND COORDINATION:

COORDINATION joins equal ideas (and, but, or, nor, for, yet, so)
SUBORDINATION makes one idea dependent on another (because, although, if, when, since, unless)

Choosing between them affects emphasis:
- "It rained, and the picnic was cancelled." (both ideas equal weight)
- "Because it rained, the picnic was cancelled." (rain is the cause; cancellation is the result)

PARALLELISM IN STRUCTURE:

Items joined by coordinating conjunctions or listed in a series must be grammatically parallel.

WITH VERBS: "She likes to sing, dance, and act" ✓ / "She likes singing, dancing, and to act" ✗
WITH NOUNS: "The job requires skill, patience, and determination" ✓
WITH CLAUSES: "He said that he was tired and that he needed rest" ✓ / "He said that he was tired and needed rest" — acceptable if the subject is the same

CORRELATIVE CONJUNCTIONS must link parallel structures:
- Both X and Y
- Either X or Y
- Neither X nor Y
- Not only X but also Y
- Whether X or Y

"Not only did she win the award, but she also received a scholarship" ✓
"Not only winning the award, but she also received a scholarship" ✗ (not parallel)

SENTENCE COMBINING AND REDUCTION:

The ACT may ask you to combine two sentences. The best combination:
- Preserves all essential information from both sentences
- Eliminates redundant words
- Uses subordination or coordination to clearly show the relationship
- Results in a smooth, grammatically correct sentence

When reducing wordy constructions:
- Relative clauses → participial phrases: "The man who was standing at the door" → "The man standing at the door"
- Passive constructions → active: "It was observed by researchers" → "Researchers observed"

APPOSITIVE PHRASES:

An appositive renames or explains the noun it follows. Set off with commas if non-restrictive:
- "My sister, a talented musician, performs every weekend."
- "The author Toni Morrison won the Nobel Prize." (no commas — the name is restrictive/essential)

ABSOLUTE PHRASES:

An absolute phrase modifies the entire sentence (not just one word). It consists of a noun + participial phrase:
- "His hands trembling, he opened the envelope."
- "The work finished, they celebrated."

These are grammatically correct and can add sophistication to writing.
