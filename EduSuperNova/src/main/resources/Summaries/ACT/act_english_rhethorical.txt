RHETORICAL SKILLS

OVERVIEW: Rhetorical skills questions on the ACT English test evaluate your ability to assess writing choices that go beyond grammar — including the effectiveness of word choices, the clarity of ideas, and the overall impact of a passage on its intended audience.

WRITER'S PURPOSE: Every effective piece of writing has a purpose. Understanding why the author wrote a passage helps you evaluate whether specific choices are appropriate.

Common purposes:
- To inform or explain (expository writing)
- To persuade or argue (argumentative writing)
- To entertain or engage (narrative or creative writing)
- To describe (descriptive writing)

A choice that serves one purpose may undermine another. An unexpected humorous anecdote might work well in a personal essay but be inappropriate in an academic argument.

SUPPORTING DETAILS AND EVIDENCE:

Strong writing uses specific, relevant details to support its claims. On the ACT, you may be asked to evaluate whether a particular detail:
- Effectively supports the paragraph's main idea
- Provides necessary specificity
- Is redundant or off-topic
- Fits the tone and purpose of the passage

IMPORTANT: More detail is not always better. A detail must be relevant AND specific to add value. Vague details ("This shows how important the topic is") add nothing.

INTRODUCTION AND CONCLUSION EFFECTIVENESS:

An effective introduction:
- Engages the reader (question, anecdote, surprising fact, vivid image)
- Establishes the topic clearly
- Sets the appropriate tone

An effective conclusion:
- Revisits the central idea without merely repeating it
- Provides closure or broader significance
- Does not introduce new, unexplained ideas
- Leaves the reader with something to think about

KEY CONCEPT: A conclusion that merely restates the introduction word-for-word is weak. A strong conclusion synthesizes and elevates — it shows what the essay as a whole has demonstrated.

WORD CHOICE FOR EFFECT:

Rhetorical skills include choosing words that not only mean the right thing but also create the right effect:
- Formal vocabulary for professional or academic contexts
- Sensory language for vivid description
- Strong verbs instead of weak verb + adverb combinations ("sprinted" vs. "ran quickly")
- Emotionally resonant words for persuasive writing

EXAMPLE: "The building was destroyed" is neutral. "The building was obliterated" is more vivid and dramatic. Which is better depends entirely on the context.

EVALUATING RHETORICAL EFFECTIVENESS:

When asked whether a passage, paragraph, or sentence accomplishes a specific goal, apply this checklist:
1. What is the stated goal?
2. Does the content address that goal directly?
3. Is the language appropriate (specific enough, toned correctly, not contradictory)?
4. Does it fit seamlessly within the surrounding context?

COMMON RHETORICAL QUESTION TYPES:

"Which choice provides the most vivid image?" → Look for sensory, concrete language
"Which choice most effectively conveys the character's frustration?" → Look for emotionally charged, specific language
"Which choice provides the most logical transition between the two ideas?" → Look for the transition that accurately reflects the relationship (cause/effect, contrast, sequence, etc.)
"Which choice best introduces the paragraph?" → Look for broad, orienting language that sets up the details that follow

THE "DOES IT HELP OR HURT?" FRAMEWORK:

For any rhetorical choice, ask:
- Does it add meaning or just add words?
- Does it fit the tone?
- Does it advance the writer's purpose?
- Would a thoughtful reader appreciate this choice, or find it confusing, redundant, or off-putting?

RHETORICAL APPEALS (background knowledge):
- ETHOS: credibility and authority of the author
- PATHOS: emotional appeal to the reader
- LOGOS: logical reasoning and evidence

The ACT won't ask you to label these, but understanding them helps you evaluate why certain word choices or details are more effective than others.

PASSAGE-LEVEL THINKING:

Many rhetorical skill questions require you to understand the essay's overall argument, theme, or narrative arc. Before diving into individual questions, take 30-45 seconds to skim the entire passage and identify:
- The topic
- The author's perspective or stance
- The general tone (formal, conversational, passionate, humorous, etc.)
- The structure (does it build an argument? Tell a story? Explain a process?)

REMEMBER: Rhetorical skills questions are about effectiveness, not just correctness. A grammatically perfect sentence can still be rhetorically weak. Always evaluate choices against the passage's specific purpose and audience.
