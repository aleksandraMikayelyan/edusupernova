READING — LITERARY NARRATIVE

OVERVIEW: The Literary Narrative passage on the ACT comes first and is always a prose fiction excerpt or personal narrative. It tests your ability to understand character, theme, tone, narrative technique, and the subtleties of literary language.

THE NATURE OF LITERARY PASSAGES:

Unlike informational passages, literary texts communicate through suggestion, implication, and subtext. What characters think, feel, and want is often shown through their actions, dialogue, and the narrator's observations — not stated outright.

KEY CONCEPT: In literary passages, your job is often to INFER rather than simply locate. Practice reading between the lines — what is the author showing you without saying it directly?

QUESTION TYPES FOR LITERARY NARRATIVE:

DETAIL QUESTIONS: Ask about specific facts stated in the passage.
- Strategy: Return to the passage and locate the specific detail. Don't rely on memory.

INFERENCE QUESTIONS: Ask what the passage implies or suggests.
- Strategy: Find the evidence in the text. The correct answer is always supported by specific textual clues.

MAIN IDEA/PURPOSE QUESTIONS: Ask about the passage's overall theme or the author's purpose.
- Strategy: Identify what the passage as a whole is about, not just one section.

VOCABULARY IN CONTEXT: Ask what a word means as used in the passage.
- Strategy: Reread the surrounding sentences. The correct meaning fits the specific context — not just the general dictionary definition.

CHARACTER QUESTIONS: Ask about a character's feelings, motivations, attitudes, or development.
- Strategy: Look for the character's direct statements, reactions, and actions. What do they DO and HOW do they react?

NARRATOR PERSPECTIVE: Ask about the narrator's point of view or attitude.
- Strategy: Identify the type of narration (first person, third limited, third omniscient) and look for the narrator's tone and word choices that reveal their perspective.

TONE AND MOOD:

TONE: The author's/narrator's attitude toward the subject.
Common tone words: nostalgic, melancholic, sardonic, reverent, detached, wistful, ironic, ominous, celebratory, ambivalent

MOOD: The emotional atmosphere the writing creates for the reader.

KEY CONCEPT: Tone is conveyed through diction (word choice), imagery, pacing, and what details the author chooses to include or emphasize. When identifying tone, look at the WORDS themselves — are they warm or cold? Formal or casual? Gentle or harsh?

NARRATIVE TECHNIQUE:

POINT OF VIEW:
- First person: "I" narrator; limited to their own knowledge and feelings; potentially unreliable
- Third person limited: narrator knows one character's thoughts
- Third person omniscient: narrator knows all characters' thoughts and motivations

IMPORTANT: A first-person narrator's perception may be biased or incomplete. The ACT sometimes asks about the LIMITATIONS of a particular narrative perspective.

STRUCTURE: How is the narrative organized?
- Chronological: events in time order
- In medias res: begins in the middle of action
- Flashback/Flashforward: time shifts
- Framing narrative: a story within a story

PACING: How quickly or slowly events unfold. Short sentences and brief paragraphs create fast pacing; longer, more elaborate sentences slow the pace and create depth.

LITERARY DEVICES:

SIMILE: comparison using "like" or "as" — "Her voice was like gravel."
METAPHOR: direct comparison — "Her voice was gravel."
PERSONIFICATION: giving human qualities to non-human things — "The wind whispered through the trees."
IMAGERY: language that appeals to the senses (sight, sound, smell, taste, touch)
SYMBOLISM: an object, action, or image that represents something beyond its literal meaning
IRONY: a gap between expectation and reality (situational), between what is said and meant (verbal), or between what a character knows and what the reader knows (dramatic)
ALLUSION: a reference to a famous work, event, or person

IMPORTANT: When a question asks about a literary device, identify what the device is, then determine what effect it creates or what it reveals about character/theme.

THEME: The central idea or message of the text. Themes are usually expressed as full statements, not just topics.
- Topic: "loss"
- Theme: "Loss can be the catalyst for unexpected growth."

VOCABULARY STRATEGY:

When asked what a word means in context:
1. Re-read the sentence and surrounding sentences
2. Substitute each answer choice into the sentence
3. The correct answer fits naturally and matches the passage's tone
4. Beware of words with multiple meanings — the most common meaning may NOT be the one used

READING EFFICIENTLY:

For Literary Narrative, the most important skill is reading with ATTENTION to feeling and implication. Skim factual passages for facts; read literary passages for atmosphere and character.

IMPORTANT: Literary questions often have two "close" options. The wrong one might be partially true or sound plausible, but won't be fully supported by the text. The right answer is always backed by SPECIFIC textual evidence.

REMEMBER: In Literary Narrative, the most common ACT trap is choosing an answer that is too extreme or too absolute. If the text shows a character is "somewhat nervous," the answer "terrified" is wrong even if fear is present. Match the intensity of your answer to the intensity in the text.
