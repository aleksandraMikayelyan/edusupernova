IELTS WRITING TASK 1 GENERAL TRAINING — LETTERS

General Training Task 1 requires you to write a letter of at least 150 words in approximately 20 minutes. The task describes a situation and gives you three bullet points of content to cover. Your response is assessed on the same four criteria as Academic Task 1: Task Achievement, Coherence and Cohesion, Lexical Resource, and Grammatical Range and Accuracy.

THE THREE LETTER TYPES

Every IELTS General Training Task 1 letter is one of three types, and this determines your register — the level of formality you use throughout.

FORMAL LETTER: Written to someone you do not know personally, usually in an official or professional capacity. Examples: a letter to a company, a local authority, a newspaper editor, a university admissions office, or a government department.

SEMI-FORMAL LETTER: Written to someone you know slightly or in a professional-personal context. Examples: a letter to your landlord, a college tutor, a manager at work, or a neighbour you know but are not close to.

INFORMAL LETTER: Written to someone you know well. Examples: a letter to a friend, a family member, or a close colleague.

KEY CONCEPT: Register must be consistent throughout your entire letter. A formal letter that begins "Dear Sir or Madam" and ends "Cheers" is inconsistent. An informal letter that opens "Hi Sarah" but uses stiff formal language in the body reads unnaturally. Inconsistent register is penalised under both Lexical Resource and Coherence.

IDENTIFYING THE REGISTER

The task prompt makes the register clear. Read it carefully for these signals:

FORMAL SIGNALS: "Write a letter to the manager of..." / "Write to the company..." / "Write a letter to the local council..." / "Write to a newspaper..."

SEMI-FORMAL SIGNALS: "Write to your landlord..." / "Write to your employer..." / "Write to your course tutor..."

INFORMAL SIGNALS: "Write to a friend..." / "Write to a family member..." / "A friend has offered to help... write to them..."

IMPORTANT: When in doubt, err toward more formal language. Inappropriate informality in a formal letter is penalised more heavily than appropriate formality in a semi-formal context. The reverse is also true — never use formal or stiff language in an informal letter.

LETTER STRUCTURE — THE FRAMEWORK

OPENING SALUTATION:
- Formal (unknown recipient): Dear Sir or Madam,
- Formal (known name): Dear Mr Smith, / Dear Ms Johnson,
- Semi-formal: Dear Mr/Ms [surname],
- Informal: Dear [first name], / Hi [first name],

OPENING PARAGRAPH: State the purpose of the letter clearly and concisely. In formal and semi-formal letters, get to the point in the first sentence. In informal letters, a brief personal greeting or reference to previous contact is appropriate before stating the reason for writing.

BODY PARAGRAPHS: One paragraph per bullet point from the task. Each paragraph develops one of the three required points. Do not merge bullet points into one paragraph and do not leave any bullet point unaddressed — failing to cover all three points significantly lowers your Task Achievement score.

CLOSING PARAGRAPH: A brief closing statement that signals the end of the letter. In formal letters: a call to action or expression of anticipation. In informal letters: a warm sign-off comment.

CLOSING SALUTATION:
- Formal (when you opened with "Dear Sir or Madam"): Yours faithfully,
- Formal (when you used a name): Yours sincerely,
- Semi-formal: Kind regards, / Best regards,
- Informal: Best wishes, / Take care, / Love, / All the best,

REMEMBER: The formal rule is absolute — "Dear Sir or Madam" MUST pair with "Yours faithfully." "Dear Mr Smith" MUST pair with "Yours sincerely." Mixing these is a register error that examiners notice immediately.

THE THREE BULLET POINTS

Every task gives three bullet points specifying the content you must include. Each should occupy its own body paragraph. The bullet points often correspond to one of three letter purposes:

EXPLAIN: "Explain why you are writing" / "Describe what happened" / "Explain the problem"
REQUEST: "Request specific information" / "Ask for something to be done" / "Suggest a solution"
RESPOND: "Say what you would like to happen" / "Propose a course of action" / "Offer to help"

KEY CONCEPT: Task Achievement requires ALL THREE bullet points to be addressed fully and specifically. A common mistake is covering all three but treating one superficially with only one vague sentence. Each bullet point deserves a full paragraph with relevant, specific detail.

GENERATING CONTENT FOR BULLET POINTS

The prompt often gives you very little detail — for example, "Explain why you need the item." You must invent realistic, plausible details to develop the bullet point into a full paragraph.

EXAMPLE: Task — "You recently lent a book to a friend. Write a letter to ask for it back. In your letter: explain why you lent them the book, explain why you now need it back, suggest a convenient time to collect it."

WEAK RESPONSE (too vague): "I lent you the book because you wanted it. I need it back because I need to use it. You can bring it on Tuesday."

STRONG RESPONSE (specific and developed): "As you may recall, I lent you my copy of 'The Psychology of Learning' last month after you mentioned you were struggling with your revision for your psychology module. I was happy to help at the time as I had already finished using it for my own studies. However, my end-of-year examinations are now approaching rapidly, and I find myself needing the book as an essential reference for my final revision sessions. It covers several topics that I expect will feature heavily in the papers. Would it be possible for you to drop the book off at my flat this coming weekend, perhaps on Saturday morning? I will be at home all day and it would be extremely convenient for me."

FORMAL LETTER VOCABULARY AND PHRASES

OPENING PHRASES:
- "I am writing to enquire about..." / "I am writing with regard to..."
- "I am writing to express my concern regarding..."
- "I am writing to complain about..." / "I wish to bring to your attention..."
- "I am writing in response to your advertisement..."
- "I would like to draw your attention to..."

REQUESTING POLITELY:
- "I would be grateful if you could..." / "I would appreciate it if..."
- "Could you please..." / "I would like to request..."
- "I would be most obliged if you could..."
- "It would be helpful if..."

EXPRESSING DISSATISFACTION (formal complaint):
- "I was extremely disappointed to discover that..."
- "I found the situation entirely unacceptable..."
- "I feel that this does not reflect the standard I was led to expect..."
- "I would urge you to address this matter as a priority."

EXPRESSING APPRECIATION:
- "I am most grateful for..." / "I would like to thank you for..."
- "Your assistance in this matter would be greatly appreciated."

CLOSING PHRASES:
- "I look forward to hearing from you at your earliest convenience."
- "I would welcome the opportunity to discuss this matter further."
- "Please do not hesitate to contact me should you require any further information."
- "I trust you will give this matter your prompt attention."

SEMI-FORMAL LETTER VOCABULARY AND PHRASES

OPENING PHRASES:
- "I am writing regarding..." / "I wanted to get in touch about..."
- "I hope this letter finds you well. I am writing to..."
- "I am writing to let you know about a situation that has arisen..."

REQUESTING:
- "I would appreciate it if you could..."
- "Would it be possible for you to...?"
- "I was hoping you might be able to..."

CLOSING PHRASES:
- "I look forward to your response."
- "Thank you for your time and consideration."
- "Please feel free to contact me if you have any questions."

INFORMAL LETTER VOCABULARY AND PHRASES

OPENING PHRASES:
- "How are you? I hope everything is going well with you."
- "It was so lovely to hear from you!"
- "I'm sorry it has taken me so long to write back — things have been incredibly busy here."
- "I've been meaning to write to you for ages!"

SHARING NEWS:
- "You'll never guess what happened to me recently..."
- "I wanted to tell you about something exciting..."
- "Something really interesting came up and I immediately thought of you."

MAKING REQUESTS INFORMALLY:
- "I was wondering if you could possibly..."
- "Would you mind helping me out with something?"
- "I know it's a big ask, but..."
- "It would mean the world to me if you could..."

CLOSING PHRASES:
- "Write back soon — I'd love to hear from you!"
- "Looking forward to catching up properly soon."
- "Give my love to [family member/mutual friend]."
- "Take care and speak soon!"

THE COMMON LETTER TYPES AND HOW TO APPROACH EACH

COMPLAINT LETTER (formal): Always: state the problem clearly → give specific details (when, what happened, what was wrong) → explain the impact on you → state what you expect the recipient to do. Tone: firm but professional, never rude or aggressive.

REQUEST FOR INFORMATION LETTER (formal/semi-formal): State what you need → explain why you need it → specify the format or deadline if relevant → thank them in advance.

APOLOGY LETTER (semi-formal/informal): Acknowledge the specific issue → take responsibility → explain (briefly — do not over-justify) → say what you will do to make it right → express genuine regret.

INVITATION LETTER (informal): Express enthusiasm → give clear details (when, where, what) → explain why you want the person to come → ask for a response.

RECOMMENDATION / REFERENCE LETTER (formal): State who you are and your relationship to the person → describe their relevant qualities with specific examples → make a clear positive recommendation → offer to provide further information.

ASKING FOR HELP LETTER (informal): Explain your situation → make the specific request → make it easy for the person to help (give details, suggest options) → express gratitude and offer to reciprocate.

GRAMMAR AND LANGUAGE NOTES

CONDITIONALS FOR POLITENESS: Formal requests use conditional structures to soften demands. "I would be grateful if you could..." is more polite than "Please send me..." The conditional is a register marker as much as a grammar choice.

PASSIVE VOICE IN FORMAL LETTERS: Passive constructions add formality and remove directness that might seem aggressive. "The order was not delivered on the agreed date" (passive) sounds more measured than "You did not deliver my order on time" (active and accusatory).

MODAL VERBS FOR TONE: "Could you...?" is more polite than "Can you...?" "Would you be able to...?" is more formal than "Will you...?" "Might it be possible to...?" is the most tentative and therefore the most formal option.

CONTRACTIONS: Formal letters never use contractions. "I am" not "I'm." "It is" not "It's." Informal letters may use contractions freely — their absence makes informal writing sound stilted and unnatural.

TOP STRATEGIES FOR GENERAL TRAINING TASK 1

Strategy: Identify the register in the first 10 seconds of reading the task. This single decision shapes every vocabulary and grammar choice that follows.

Strategy: Plan one paragraph per bullet point before writing. Note two or three specific details you will invent for each one. This prevents vague, underdeveloped paragraphs.

Strategy: Check your opening and closing salutations pair correctly before submitting. This is a simple, binary rule that many candidates violate unnecessarily.

Strategy: After writing, review your entire letter for register consistency. Every sentence should feel like it belongs to the same register.

Strategy: Aim for approximately 170-180 words to comfortably exceed the 150-word minimum with enough detail. Going significantly over 200 words wastes time that belongs to Task 2.

COMMON MISTAKES TO AVOID

- Pairing "Dear Sir or Madam" with "Yours sincerely" — must be "Yours faithfully"
- Using informal contractions in a formal letter
- Leaving one bullet point underdeveloped with only a single vague sentence
- Failing to invent specific details — vague generalities score poorly for Task Achievement
- Maintaining a formal tone in an informal letter — this is as wrong as the reverse
- Writing bullet points as a list rather than as developed paragraphs
- Beginning informal letters without any personal greeting or connection
- Omitting the closing salutation entirely
