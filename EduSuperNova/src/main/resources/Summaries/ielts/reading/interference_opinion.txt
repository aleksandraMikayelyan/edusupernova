INFERENCE AND OPINION IN IELTS READING

Inference and opinion questions go beyond locating explicit facts. They require you to understand what a writer implies, believes, or suggests — information present in the text but not directly stated. These appear in YES/NO/NOT GIVEN questions, multiple choice questions, and certain matching tasks. They are among the most challenging questions in IELTS Reading.

WHAT INFERENCE MEANS IN IELTS

INFERENCE: A conclusion that follows logically and necessarily from information in the text, even though it is not explicitly stated.

IMPORTANT DISTINCTION: An IELTS inference is not a creative interpretation. It is the most direct, most cautious logical conclusion that the text's information demands. If you need to add assumptions, apply outside knowledge, or take a large logical leap, the inference is too large.

THE INFERENCE TEST: Before choosing an inference as an answer, ask: "If everything in this passage is true, does this conclusion HAVE to be true?" If the answer is yes — or very nearly yes — it is a valid IELTS inference. If it only might be true, or could be true under certain conditions, it is too speculative.

KEY CONCEPT: IELTS inference questions are conservative by design. The correct answer is almost always the most modest, most directly supported conclusion available. Between a bold inference and a cautious one, choose the cautious one.

YES / NO / NOT GIVEN FOR OPINIONS

This question format appears when the passage expresses an author's VIEW, ARGUMENT, or CLAIM rather than pure fact. The three answers:

YES: The statement reflects the writer's view as expressed in the passage.
NO: The statement contradicts the writer's expressed view.
NOT GIVEN: The writer does not address this point — no view is expressed either way.

CRITICAL DIFFERENCE FROM TRUE/FALSE/NOT GIVEN: In YES/NO/NOT GIVEN, you are evaluating whether the writer BELIEVES or ARGUES something, not whether a fact is stated. A passage might state facts about climate change (TRUE/FALSE/NG) but also contain the author's interpretive conclusions (YES/NO/NG).

NOTE: NOT GIVEN here means the author simply has not commented on this aspect. It does not mean the claim is factually wrong or uncertain.

IDENTIFYING AUTHORIAL OPINION IN ACADEMIC TEXTS

Academic writers rarely say "I believe" or "I think." They signal opinion through more subtle language choices.

STRONG CLAIM LANGUAGE (author asserts this confidently):
- "It is clear that..." / "It is evident that..."
- "The evidence demonstrates that..." / "This confirms that..."
- "There is no doubt that..." / "Clearly..."
- "It must be concluded that..."

MODERATE CLAIM LANGUAGE (author argues this but acknowledges uncertainty):
- "The evidence suggests that..." / "It appears that..."
- "It is likely that..." / "This may indicate..."
- "Research points to..." / "It can be argued that..."

WEAK CLAIM LANGUAGE / HEDGING (author is cautious or non-committal):
- "It is possible that..." / "Some researchers believe..."
- "There is some evidence to suggest..." / "It might be the case that..."
- "It is not clear whether..." / "This remains uncertain."

CRITICAL LANGUAGE (author expresses a negative or sceptical view):
- "Despite claims to the contrary..." / "Critics argue..."
- "The evidence fails to support..." / "This assumption is flawed..."
- "Contrary to popular belief..." / "This view overlooks..."

KEY CONCEPT: The STRENGTH of the author's claim matters as much as its direction. "The author might believe X" and "the author is convinced that X" are very different. Questions will test whether you recognised the degree of conviction, not just the topic.

RECOGNISING IMPLICIT MEANING

Writers use several techniques to convey meaning without stating it directly.

IMPLICATION THROUGH CONTRAST: By describing one option negatively, the author implicitly endorses the alternative. "Traditional approaches have consistently failed to address the underlying causes. New models focus on systemic change." Implication: the author favours new models, though this is never stated.

IMPLICATION THROUGH SELECTION: The author's choice of which evidence to include reveals their position. A passage that cites five studies supporting one conclusion and only briefly mentions one study on the other side implies the author finds the evidence unequal.

IMPLICATION THROUGH EVALUATIVE VOCABULARY: "The simplistic assumption that..." implies the author finds this view inadequate. "The elegant solution proposed by..." implies the author admires it. These evaluative adjectives carry implicit opinion.

IMPLICATION THROUGH RHETORICAL QUESTION: "Can we afford to dismiss these findings?" implies the author believes we cannot.

IMPLICATION THROUGH CONCESSION STRUCTURE: "While it is true that X has some merit, the more significant point is Y." The concession ("some merit") is minimal — the real endorsement is Y.

IMPORTANT: Implication is always present in the text. Correct inference questions never require you to leave the text — the clue for the correct answer always exists within it, even if subtly. If you cannot identify specific textual evidence, your inference is likely speculation.

MULTIPLE CHOICE INFERENCE QUESTIONS

Multiple choice questions in IELTS Reading sometimes test inference directly: "What does the writer imply about X?" or "Which of the following can be inferred from paragraph 3?"

APPROACH:

Step 1: Read the question and identify what is being asked — whose opinion or what implication is being tested.

Step 2: Return to the relevant passage section. Read it carefully with the question in mind.

Step 3: For each answer option, ask: "Is this directly implied by the text, or am I adding my own reasoning?"

Step 4: Eliminate answers that are: too strong (go further than the text warrants), contradicted by the text, or require outside knowledge.

Step 5: Choose the answer most directly and conservatively supported by the text.

DISTRACTOR TYPES FOR INFERENCE/OPINION QUESTIONS:

TOO STRONG: "The writer believes that all traditional methods are ineffective." — The passage only criticised one specific method. This overgeneralises.

CONTRADICTED: "The writer is optimistic about the technology's immediate application." — When the passage expresses caution about immediate rollout. Students sometimes miss qualifiers like "immediate."

OUTSIDE KNOWLEDGE: "This approach has been confirmed by subsequent research." — The passage doesn't say this; the student added it from general knowledge.

PLAUSIBLE BUT ABSENT: "The writer suggests the solution would be cost-effective." — The passage never addresses cost. This sounds reasonable but is NOT GIVEN.

WRITER'S PURPOSE QUESTIONS

"Why does the writer mention X in paragraph 3?" / "What is the purpose of the example in the second paragraph?"

These questions test inference about authorial intent — why information was included, not just what it says.

COMMON AUTHORIAL PURPOSES:
- To provide evidence for the preceding claim
- To introduce a contrast or counterexample
- To illustrate a concept with a concrete case
- To refute or challenge an opposing view
- To acknowledge a limitation of the argument
- To establish historical context
- To show the scale or significance of the issue
- To define a technical term through example

KEY CONCEPT: Purpose is about FUNCTION in the argument, not CONTENT. "The writer mentions the Amazon rainforest to illustrate..." is a purpose answer. "The writer mentions the Amazon rainforest because it contains..." is a content answer. Always ask: what role does this play in the text's logic?

DISTINGUISHING AUTHOR'S VIEW FROM REPORTED VIEWS

Academic writers frequently report the views of others — researchers, critics, historical figures, institutions. These reported views are not the author's own opinion.

REPORTED VIEW SIGNALS:
- "According to Smith (2008)..." / "Jones argues that..."
- "Some researchers claim..." / "Critics contend that..."
- "The government maintained that..." / "The traditional view holds that..."
- "It has been suggested that..." (by others, not the author)

AUTHOR'S OWN VIEW SIGNALS:
- "However, this view overlooks..." / "This argument fails to account for..."
- "A more nuanced understanding suggests..." / "What the evidence actually demonstrates is..."
- "The most compelling explanation is..." / "It is clear that..."

IMPORTANT: A YES/NO/NOT GIVEN question about the author's view requires you to identify what the AUTHOR believes, not what a cited researcher believes. Confusing a reported view with the author's own position is among the most common errors on YES/NO/NOT GIVEN questions.

EXAMPLE: A passage reports that "early economists argued that the market would self-regulate naturally." The author then writes: "This optimistic view, however, proved tragically mistaken." A question asking "Does the author believe the market self-regulates?" — answer: NO. The author clearly rejects this view, even though they reported it neutrally first.

TONE AS EVIDENCE FOR OPINION QUESTIONS

TONE: The attitude conveyed by the writer's language choices across the passage.

OBJECTIVE/NEUTRAL TONE: Balanced presentation, hedged language, no strong evaluative adjectives. The writer presents multiple views without clearly endorsing one. Common in scientific reports.

PERSUASIVE/ARGUMENTATIVE TONE: The writer builds a case for a specific position. Evidence is selected and presented in ways that support the conclusion. Claims are made with confidence.

CRITICAL TONE: The writer challenges, disputes, or finds fault. Evaluative adjectives are negative. Counterarguments are cited only to be dismissed.

APPRECIATIVE TONE: The writer admires or endorses. Evaluative adjectives are positive. Contributions are praised.

KEY CONCEPT: Tone is cumulative — it builds across paragraphs. A single positive adjective does not define the tone. Look at the overall pattern. What does the writer say at the start, middle, and end? Does their language lean positive, negative, or neutral overall?

TOP STRATEGIES FOR INFERENCE AND OPINION QUESTIONS

Strategy: For YES/NO/NOT GIVEN, locate the exact passage section relating to the question before deciding. Never answer from memory alone.

Strategy: Look for evaluative and hedging vocabulary — these are the clearest signals of author opinion and confidence level.

Strategy: Distinguish carefully between what the author says and what the author reports other people saying. The signal verbs (argues, claims, contends, maintains) identify reported speech.

Strategy: For inference questions, choose the most conservative option. Bold inferences are almost always wrong on IELTS.

Strategy: For purpose questions, ask "what work does this section do in the text's argument?" rather than "what does this section say?"

Strategy: When a question feels like NOT GIVEN, scan the entire relevant section one more time before committing. Writers sometimes address a point briefly where you might not expect it.

COMMON MISTAKES TO AVOID

- Confusing NOT GIVEN with NO — NOT GIVEN means the text is silent; NO means the text contradicts
- Using personal knowledge or logic to infer what the author "probably" believes
- Treating a reported view as the author's own opinion
- Over-inferring: choosing an inference that goes beyond what the text strictly implies
- Missing evaluative vocabulary that signals author attitude because it seems minor
- Answering YES when the author only reported someone else's positive claim, without endorsing it
- Selecting an inference answer based on the topic being familiar, rather than on what this specific passage says
