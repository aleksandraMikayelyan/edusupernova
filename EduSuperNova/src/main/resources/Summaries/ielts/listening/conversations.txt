IELTS LISTENING CONVERSATIONS

Conversations appear in Sections 1 and 3 of the IELTS Listening test. Section 1 is a practical everyday dialogue between two people. Section 3 is an academic or educational discussion between two to four speakers — typically students, a tutor, or a mix. Both require you to follow a dynamic back-and-forth exchange, track multiple speakers, and capture specific information accurately.

SECTION 1 CONVERSATIONS

WHAT TO EXPECT: Two speakers in a real-world transactional context. One speaker usually needs information or a service; the other provides it.

COMMON SITUATIONS:
- Enrolling in a course or activity
- Booking accommodation, travel, or tickets
- Registering for a library card or membership
- Making an appointment or reservation
- Enquiring about a rental property or community service
- Reporting a lost item or filing a complaint

REGISTER: Informal to semi-formal. Language is everyday English, but answers require precise detail: full names, addresses, phone numbers, dates, prices, and reference codes.

KEY CONCEPT: Section 1 is designed to be the most accessible section. It moves at a conversational pace, contains a break in the middle (a brief pause between parts), and focuses on factual detail rather than complex ideas. Many students lose points here through careless spelling and number errors — not comprehension failures. Be precise.

SECTION 1 ANSWER TYPES

FORM AND NOTE COMPLETION: The most common Section 1 format. A form (booking form, registration card, enquiry sheet) has gaps to fill. Answers are usually: names, numbers, addresses, times, prices, or short descriptive words.

SPELLING: The speaker will often spell out names, streets, and codes. Be ready to write letters as you hear them. Practice the IELTS phonetic alphabet: "B as in bravo," "C as in Charlie."

NUMBERS: Phone numbers, postcodes, reference numbers. Write them exactly as spoken. "0-7-double 3-9-4" = 07339 4. "Flat 4B" is not the same as "Flat 4b" — capitalise as the speaker indicates.

PRICES: Write in numerals with currency symbol if needed. "Forty-five pounds fifty" = £45.50.

DATES: Write in the format that fits the blank. "The fourteenth of March" = 14th March or 14 March (both acceptable). "Two thousand and twenty-four" = 2024.

IMPORTANT: The recording in Section 1 often includes distractors — the speaker offers one detail and then corrects it. "The class starts at six... actually, let me check... yes, it's six thirty." Always write the FINAL, confirmed information. The distractor technique is most common in Section 1.

SECTION 3 CONVERSATIONS

WHAT TO EXPECT: A discussion between two to four speakers in an academic context, usually university students working on a project or assignment, sometimes joined by a tutor.

COMMON SITUATIONS:
- Two students planning a research project or presentation
- A student consulting a tutor or academic advisor
- A group of students discussing data from an experiment
- Students comparing their interpretations of course material
- A tutor providing feedback on an essay or report

REGISTER: Semi-formal academic English. Speakers use hedging, agreement, polite disagreement, and academic vocabulary. The discussion is more complex than Section 1.

KEY CONCEPT: Section 3 tests your ability to follow a discussion where opinions may differ, topics shift, and speakers interrupt or build on each other's ideas. Tracking WHO says WHAT is as important as noting WHAT was said.

SPEAKER TRACKING IN SECTION 3

Confusing which speaker said something is the most common error in Section 3. Use a simple system from the moment the recording begins.

If there are two speakers: Label your notes with initials or S1/S2.
If there are three speakers: S1, S2, S3 from first introduction.
Write each speaker's key statements next to their label.

AGREEMENT AND DISAGREEMENT LANGUAGE:

Agreement:
- "Exactly." / "That's a good point." / "I agree with you on that."
- "Yes, and I think we should also..." (agreement + addition)
- "Right, so what we're saying is..."

Partial agreement:
- "I see what you mean, but..." (partially disagrees)
- "That's true to an extent, although..."
- "Yes, though I'm not sure that..."

Disagreement:
- "I'm not convinced that..." / "I don't think that's quite right."
- "Actually, I think the opposite might be true."
- "Surely that can't be the only factor."

NOTE: The IELTS specifically tests whether you can distinguish genuine agreement from polite partial disagreement. "I suppose you could look at it that way" is NOT agreement — it is diplomatic reluctance. Questions will ask what a speaker actually thinks, not what they politely acknowledged.

QUESTION TYPES IN SECTIONS 1 AND 3

FORM COMPLETION: Most common in Section 1. Fixed number of words allowed.

TABLE OR NOTE COMPLETION: Used in both sections. A table compares options or organizes information from the discussion.

MULTIPLE CHOICE: "What does the student decide to do?" / "Which point do both speakers agree on?" Choose from A, B, or C.

MATCHING: Match a list of items (e.g., topics, people, options) to a list of qualities or descriptions. Used frequently in Section 3.

MAP OR PLAN LABELLING: Match letters on a map or diagram to place names from the conversation. Common in Section 1.

SENTENCE OR SUMMARY COMPLETION: Complete sentences based on the conversation's content.

IMPORTANT: Matching questions in Section 3 require you to track which speaker's opinion matches each option. Questions like "which speaker thinks the deadline is reasonable?" require careful speaker tracking. Wrong answers often correctly represent one speaker's opinion but attribute it to the wrong person.

FOLLOWING TOPIC SHIFTS IN SECTION 3

Academic discussions regularly shift topics. Signals to watch for:

TOPIC INTRODUCTION: "So, moving on to the data analysis..." / "Let's talk about the methodology next."

TOPIC RETURN: "Going back to what you said earlier..." / "Coming back to the main argument..."

OPINION INTRODUCTION: "Personally, I think..." / "From my perspective..." / "In my view..."

CONCESSION: "You make a fair point, though I still think..." / "I hadn't considered that, but..."

KEY CONCEPT: When a speaker concedes a point and then immediately restates their position, the FINAL statement is their TRUE opinion. IELTS questions often test the speaker's opinion AFTER the concession, not before it.

DISTRACTORS IN CONVERSATIONS

IELTS Listening uses distractors deliberately throughout Sections 1 and 3.

TYPE 1 — SELF-CORRECTION: "It costs thirty dollars — no, wait, it's thirty-five." Write the final figure.

TYPE 2 — REJECTED OPTION: "We could do the survey in the library, but actually the cafeteria would be better for access." The correct answer is cafeteria, not library.

TYPE 3 — MENTIONED BUT NOT CHOSEN: Speaker A suggests something, Speaker B disagrees, and they settle on a third option. Only the final agreed-upon choice is correct.

TYPE 4 — STATED BUT DENIED: "I know it looks like the deadline is Thursday, but actually it's been moved to Friday." Deadline is Friday.

REMEMBER: Every distractor is introduced intentionally. The IELTS rewards listeners who hear the full exchange, not those who grab the first matching word. Always follow the conversation to its conclusion before writing your answer.

VOCABULARY FOR SECTION 1 (PRACTICAL TRANSACTIONS)

DEPOSIT: An initial payment held as security. "There's a £50 refundable deposit."
AVAILABILITY: Whether something is free or accessible. "Let me check availability for that date."
CONFIRMATION: Proof of a booking or agreement. "You'll receive email confirmation within 24 hours."
ENQUIRY: A request for information. "I'm calling with an enquiry about your courses."
CANCELLATION POLICY: Rules about cancelling a booking. "Our cancellation policy requires 48 hours' notice."
SURCHARGE: An extra fee. "A 10% surcharge applies to weekend bookings."
COMPLIMENTARY: Free of charge, included in the price. "Breakfast is complimentary."
INCLUSIVE: Included in the stated price. "All prices are inclusive of tax."

VOCABULARY FOR SECTION 3 (ACADEMIC DISCUSSIONS)

METHODOLOGY: The system of methods used in research.
HYPOTHESIS: A proposed explanation to be tested.
EMPIRICAL: Based on observation or experiment.
CRITERION (pl. CRITERIA): A standard or principle for judging.
ANALYSIS: Detailed examination of data or argument.
FEASIBILITY: Whether something can practically be done.
SCOPE: The range or extent of a topic or study.
IMPLICATION: A conclusion that can be drawn from evidence.
PEER REVIEW: Evaluation of research by other experts in the field.
DRAFT: A preliminary version of a document.

TOP STRATEGIES FOR IELTS LISTENING CONVERSATIONS

Strategy: In Section 1, use the break in the middle to read ahead to the second set of questions. Do not review your previous answers during this break.

Strategy: Listen for self-corrections and topic changes — they almost always signal that a distractor has just been introduced and the real answer is coming.

Strategy: In Section 3, mark speaker labels on your answer sheet before the recording begins so you can attribute statements instantly.

Strategy: For matching questions, read all the options before the recording begins. This lets you recognise each option when it appears rather than searching for it under time pressure.

Strategy: For map and plan questions, familiarise yourself with the diagram during pre-listening time. Trace the likely routes and note which positions are already labelled.

COMMON MISTAKES TO AVOID

- Writing a name or word you recognised early without waiting for the speaker to confirm or correct it
- Confusing Speaker A's opinion with Speaker B's in Section 3 matching questions
- Spelling names or codes incorrectly when they were clearly spelled out in the recording
- Not capitalising proper nouns (names, places, titles)
- Losing your place in the question order and writing an answer in the wrong blank
- Writing more words than the word limit allows — extra words make the answer wrong even if the correct word is included
