SPECIFIC DETAILS IN IELTS LISTENING

Specific detail questions are the most common question type across all four IELTS Listening sections. Whether you are completing a form, filling notes, labelling a map, or answering a multiple choice question, you are almost always hunting for a precise, concrete piece of information. Mastering this skill means training your ear to lock onto the right word at the right moment and transfer it accurately.

WHAT SPECIFIC DETAIL MEANS

SPECIFIC DETAIL: An exact, verifiable piece of information — a name, number, date, place, time, price, spelling, category, or short description — that appears once in the recording and corresponds to a gap or question on your answer sheet.

DETAIL VS. MAIN IDEA: The main idea tells you what the recording is about. The specific detail tells you a precise fact within it. Both matter, but for completion tasks, detail accuracy is everything.

KEY CONCEPT: Every mark in IELTS Listening that requires a written answer — note completion, form completion, table completion, sentence completion, short answer — is a specific detail question. These question types make up the vast majority of the test. Getting your detail-catching skill to near-perfection has the greatest impact on your score.

THE FIVE CATEGORIES OF SPECIFIC DETAILS

CATEGORY 1 — NAMES AND PROPER NOUNS

Includes: People's names, place names, institution names, brand names, street names, and titles.

These are always spelled out or made phonetically clear in the recording because the test cannot expect you to spell unfamiliar names from hearing alone.

LISTEN FOR: The speaker slowing down, the other speaker repeating the name back, or explicit spelling ("That's K-I-R-B-Y, Kirby").

WRITE: Exactly as spelled. Capitalise properly. "kirby" is wrong. "Kirby" is correct.

EXAMPLE: "Could I take your name, please?" / "It's Whitmore — W-H-I-T-M-O-R-E."

IMPORTANT: Write each letter as you hear it. Do not wait until the spelling is complete to start writing — by then you may have forgotten the first letters.

CATEGORY 2 — NUMBERS AND QUANTITIES

Includes: Phone numbers, postcodes, prices, dates, years, times, measurements, percentages, reference numbers, quantities.

PHONE NUMBERS: Written as spoken. "Oh" in British English = 0. "Double four" = 44. Write: 07744... not 0-7-7-4-4.

PRICES: "Thirty-five fifty" = £35.50. "Five pounds" = £5.00. Include currency symbol only if the question format clearly needs it.

TIMES: "Half past nine" = 9:30. "Quarter to three" = 2:45. Write in standard time format unless instructed otherwise.

DATES: "The twenty-second of April" = 22 April or 22nd April. Either is acceptable. "Two thousand and nineteen" = 2019.

MEASUREMENTS: Write the numeral and unit together: "4.5 kilometres" not "four point five kms."

PERCENTAGES AND FRACTIONS: Write as numerals: 35% not thirty-five percent. One word and/or a number counts as one slot in word-count limits.

REMEMBER: Numbers are the easiest detail to mishear under pressure. Practice active number listening — say the number back to yourself mentally as you write it. This quick verification step catches transposition errors (writing 46 when you heard 64).

CATEGORY 3 — TIME REFERENCES AND SEQUENCES

Includes: When something happens, how long it lasts, in what order steps occur, and the relationship between events in time.

ABSOLUTE TIME: "The exhibition opens on Monday the 3rd" — write the day and date.
RELATIVE TIME: "Three weeks after registration closes" — write "3 weeks after registration."
DURATION: "The session lasts approximately two hours" — write "2 hours."
FREQUENCY: "Meetings are held every fortnight" — write "fortnightly" or "every 2 weeks."
SEQUENCE: "First you complete the form, then attend the induction, finally submit your ID" — note the order.

KEY CONCEPT: Flow chart and process diagram questions are entirely about sequence and time. When you see these question types, your listening priority is order — not just content. A correct detail in the wrong position in a process answer is marked wrong.

CATEGORY 4 — DESCRIPTIVE DETAILS

Includes: Colours, sizes, materials, conditions, quantities described qualitatively, and short characterisations.

EXAMPLE: "The bag is a large, dark blue holdall" → answer: dark blue / large / holdall depending on what the gap requires.

ADJECTIVES AND COMPOUND NOUNS: Pay attention to whether the gap needs an adjective (describing word) or a noun (naming word). "The walls are painted ___ [colour]" needs an adjective. "The room is used as a ___" needs a noun.

NOTE: If the answer is a compound noun ("bus stop," "car park," "waiting room"), write both words. Hyphenation varies — write the most natural form and it will be accepted.

CATEGORY 5 — LOCATIONS AND DIRECTIONS

Includes: Which floor, which room number, which area of a building or map, which street or district, spatial relationships (next to, opposite, between, behind, in front of).

MAP AND PLAN LABELLING: The recording describes where something is relative to landmarks. You match letters on a map to place names from the audio.

LISTEN FOR: Directional language: "If you turn left at the main entrance and continue past the café, the seminar room is the second door on your right."

WRITE: The letter or the name as required. Do not guess based on visual plausibility — always wait for the audio to confirm.

IMPORTANT: Map questions are solved by following directions in sequence. Start from the reference point named in the recording, not from the first item in your question list. Let the recording lead you through the map.

DISTRACTOR TECHNIQUES FOR DETAIL QUESTIONS

The IELTS uses four main distractor techniques to prevent correct answering by first-word capture.

SELF-CORRECTION DISTRACTOR: "The fee is forty pounds — actually, I need to check that — it's forty-five pounds." → Write 45/£45, not 40/£40.

CHANGE-OF-PLAN DISTRACTOR: "We originally thought Friday would work, but we've moved it to Thursday." → Write Thursday, not Friday.

REJECTED-OPTION DISTRACTOR: "There's the park, the library, or the sports centre. Given the weather, the sports centre is probably best." → Write sports centre, not park or library.

NEGATIVE DISTRACTOR: "The session does NOT run on Wednesdays — only Tuesdays and Thursdays." → Write Tuesday/Thursday. Do not write Wednesday.

KEY CONCEPT: Every distractor follows the same logic: the wrong answer appears first and sounds plausible, and the correct answer appears second as a correction, revision, or selection. Train yourself to follow conversations to their conclusion rather than recording the first plausible match.

SPELLING ACCURACY — THE SILENT SCORE KILLER

Correct content in an incorrectly spelled word is marked WRONG on IELTS. This is the most preventable source of score loss on the test.

HIGH-RISK SPELLING CATEGORIES:

Double letters: committee, accommodation, necessary, beginning, parallel, occurred, referred.
Silent letters: psychology, knowledge, receipt, knight, wreck.
-ence vs. -ance: existence, independence, significance, performance, tolerance.
-tion vs. -sion: permission, possession, pronunciation, description, solution.
ie vs. ei: receive, believe, achieve, perceive, ceiling.
-ible vs. -able: responsible, accessible, available, comfortable, considerable.

STRATEGY: When the recording spells out a word, write each letter as you hear it. When it does not, and you are unsure of spelling, write your best attempt — a nearly correct spelling may be accepted on IELTS, but a completely wrong spelling never will.

IMPORTANT: Use the transfer time at the end of the test (2 minutes on paper-based IELTS) to check spelling on every answer you wrote. Focus especially on proper nouns, double letters, and -tion/-sion endings.

WORD COUNT RULES — NON-NEGOTIABLE

IELTS specifies the maximum word count for each completion question. These limits are absolute.

"ONE WORD ONLY": Write exactly one word. "dark blue" would be wrong if the answer is simply "blue." "Swimming pool" (one compound noun) counts as two words. Articles (a, an, the) count as words.

"ONE WORD AND/OR A NUMBER": You may write one word, one number, or one word plus one number. "approximately 200" is valid. "about 200 kilometres" is not (three items).

"NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS": Up to two words, but one is also acceptable. "bus stop" = two words, valid. "the bus stop" = three words, invalid.

"NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER": Up to three words and/or a number in any combination.

REMEMBER: Contractions count as one word. "Don't" = one word. Hyphenated words are generally counted as one word. When in doubt, write fewer words — a shorter accurate answer is always better than a longer inaccurate one.

ACTIVE LISTENING TECHNIQUES FOR DETAIL CAPTURE

PREDICTION: Before the recording begins, predict the answer type for each blank. If the gap is "the ___ building," you need an adjective or noun. If it is "built in ___," you need a year. Pre-activating your expectation makes the answer easier to recognise.

SYNONYM AWARENESS: IELTS often paraphrases question language in the recording. The question says "cost of accommodation" but the recording says "the rent for the rooms." Train yourself to recognise paraphrase so you don't wait for the exact question words.

CHUNKING: When listening for numbers, process them in chunks. Phone numbers come in groups. Addresses come as number + street + suburb. Train your ear to chunk, not to capture individual digits under panic.

ANCHORING: When you confirm one answer, it anchors your position in the recording. From that anchor, you know approximately how far into the recording you are and what is coming next.

NOTE: If you hear a signal phrase like "and the reference number is..." or "you'll need to bring your..." — maximise attention immediately. These intro phrases almost always precede a specific detail answer.

PRACTICE TECHNIQUES FOR DETAIL ACCURACY

DICTATION PRACTICE: Listen to short audio clips and transcribe them word-for-word. This trains your ear for speed, accent, and connected speech at the detail level.

NUMBER DICTATION: Have a study partner or audio resource read sequences of phone numbers, prices, and dates while you write them. Check accuracy. Aim for 100%.

SPELLING LISTS: Create and regularly review a personal list of words you have misspelled on practice tests. These are your personal high-risk words.

SECTION 1 FOCUS SESSIONS: Practice full Section 1 form-completion tasks at full speed. Section 1 is the highest-density specific detail section and the best training ground for all detail-capture skills.

TOP STRATEGIES FOR SPECIFIC DETAIL QUESTIONS

Strategy: Read all question gaps before the recording begins. Know exactly what type of detail you are hunting for in each gap.

Strategy: Never write an answer based on the first plausible word you hear. Follow the exchange to its conclusion.

Strategy: Write lightly during the recording so you can erase and correct when speakers self-correct.

Strategy: Use the 2-minute transfer time for spelling checks. Read every answer aloud in your head and visualise the letters.

Strategy: When you miss an answer, write your best guess immediately. A guess has a chance of earning a mark. A blank is guaranteed zero.

COMMON MISTAKES TO AVOID

- Writing the distractor instead of the corrected information
- Exceeding the word count limit — extra words invalidate the answer even if the key word is included
- Misspelling proper nouns that were clearly spelled out in the recording
- Writing "thirty-five" instead of "35" when a numeral would be more appropriate
- Leaving blanks instead of guessing when uncertain
- Writing an answer to the wrong question because you lost your place in the question sequence
- Ignoring articles and prepositions in word counts (they DO count toward the limit)
