READING DAILY LIFE AND CAMPUS SITUATIONS

Daily life and campus reading passages appear primarily in TOEFL Integrated tasks, not as standalone academic reading passages. They simulate real university written materials: announcements, notices, letters, and policy statements. Understanding this format deeply is essential for success in Integrated Writing Task 1 and Integrated Speaking Tasks 1 and 2.

WHAT THESE TEXTS ARE

These are written documents that a real university student might encounter on a bulletin board, official website, or email. They are shorter than academic passages (typically 100 to 200 words) and written in a semi-formal, direct style. The language is clear and practical.

CONTENT THEMES

FACILITIES: Library renovation, gym closures, dormitory policy updates, cafeteria changes.
SCHEDULES: Changes to registration deadlines, exam period adjustments, event cancellations.
ACADEMIC POLICIES: New grading criteria, attendance requirements, academic integrity updates.
FINANCIAL AND ADMINISTRATIVE: Tuition increases, scholarship deadline changes, fee structure updates.
COMMUNITY AND EVENTS: Campus volunteer programs, sustainability initiatives, cultural events.

THE ANATOMY OF A CAMPUS ANNOUNCEMENT

Every TOEFL campus announcement follows a reliable structure. Learn to identify each component in under 30 seconds.

THE ANNOUNCEMENT HEADING: Names the issuing office or body and the intended audience. Example: "To: All Students / From: Office of Housing and Residential Life / Re: Updated Quiet Hours Policy."

THE PROPOSAL OR CHANGE: A specific, clearly stated change that is about to happen or is being proposed. This is always the most important element. Write it in your notes in one sentence.

REASON ONE: The first justification for the change. Usually introduced by "first," "one reason," or simply stated as the first explanatory sentence. Always tested.

REASON TWO: The second justification. Usually introduced by "second," "additionally," "moreover," or "another reason." Always tested.

THE CALL TO ACTION: What students are expected to do in response. "For more information, contact..." / "Please submit your feedback by..." / "All students must comply by..."

KEY CONCEPT: In Integrated tasks, knowing this five-part structure lets you extract the two reasons instantly. Since the student's spoken response will always address these two reasons (either supporting or opposing them), your ability to identify them directly determines your integrated score.

EXAMPLE ANNOUNCEMENT (FULLY ANNOTATED)

To: All Students
From: University Dining Services
Re: Upcoming Changes to Meal Plan Pricing

[THE CHANGE] Effective next semester, the standard meal plan will increase in price by 12%.

[REASON ONE] First, the additional revenue will fund a comprehensive upgrade of cafeteria equipment and food preparation facilities, which have not been renovated in over a decade.

[REASON TWO] Second, the university intends to extend cafeteria hours to include late-night service until midnight on weekdays, providing students with greater flexibility.

[CALL TO ACTION] Students with questions or concerns are encouraged to attend the open forum on the 15th or submit written feedback via the student portal.

IMPORTANT: The TOEFL listening that follows this reading will almost always feature a student who DISAGREES with the announcement and CHALLENGES each reason. This is the standard format for Integrated Task 1 in Writing and Task 2 in Speaking.

READING LETTERS AND EDITORIALS

Some Daily Life texts take the form of letters to the editor or student op-ed pieces. These express a personal opinion about a campus issue. Their structure differs slightly:

OPENING POSITION: The writer states their stance directly in the first sentence.
REASON ONE: First supporting argument.
REASON TWO: Second supporting argument.
(OPTIONAL COUNTERARGUMENT): The writer may acknowledge opposing views before dismissing them.
CLOSING CALL: A recommendation or request for action.

NOTE: If the reading is a letter expressing an OPINION, the listening that follows may feature a student who agrees OR disagrees. When the reading is a neutral announcement, the student almost always disagrees with at least one aspect.

VOCABULARY IN CAMPUS TEXTS

IMPLEMENT: To put a plan into effect. "The policy will be implemented next month."

LOGISTICS: The practical coordination of a complex undertaking. "The logistics of the renovation require temporary closure."

POLICY: An official rule or course of action. "The new policy takes effect immediately."

FEEDBACK: Responses or reactions from stakeholders. "The administration welcomes student feedback."

PROVISIONAL: Temporary or subject to change. "This is a provisional timetable."

ELIGIBLE: Meeting the required conditions. "Eligible students may apply for a waiver."

MANDATORY: Required, not optional. "Attendance at the orientation is mandatory."

REVISE: To update or amend. "The board has revised its position following the survey."

ALLOCATE: To assign for a specific purpose. "Funds will be allocated to improve lab facilities."

FACILITATE: To make something easier. "Extended hours will facilitate better student access."

COMPLIANCE: Acting in accordance with rules or requirements. "Compliance with the new policy is expected."

WAIVER: An official document releasing someone from an obligation. "Fee waivers are available for demonstrated need."

SEMI-FORMAL LANGUAGE PATTERNS

Campus texts use semi-formal language — professional but not as dense as academic texts.

FORMAL PHRASES TO RECOGNIZE:
- "Effective [date]..." = starting from this date
- "In accordance with..." = following the rules of
- "As per..." = according to
- "Please be advised that..." = formal warning or notice
- "We regret to inform you that..." = apologetic bad news
- "In an effort to..." = introducing the reason for a change
- "Subject to change" = may be modified
- "For your convenience..." = intended to help you

CONNECTING READING TO SPEAKING AND WRITING TASKS

In Integrated Speaking Task 2 (campus announcement + conversation):
- Your 60-second response summarizes the student's opinion and two reasons
- The student's reasons directly challenge the two reasons in the announcement
- Example structure: "The announcement states that [change] because [reason 1] and [reason 2]. The student disagrees. First, [student's counterpoint to reason 1]. Second, [student's counterpoint to reason 2]."

In Integrated Writing Task 1 (academic reading + lecture):
- The announcement format appears less often; this task more commonly pairs academic texts with academic lectures
- When it does appear: same structure applies — identify the change, identify both reasons, and note how the lecture (student speaker) responds to each

STRATEGY FOR DAILY LIFE READING: THE WHO-WHAT-WHEN-WHY-HOW FRAMEWORK

WHO: Which office or authority is issuing this? Who is the audience?
WHAT: What is the specific change or proposal?
WHEN: When does it take effect?
WHY: What are the two stated reasons?
HOW: What action is required of students?

Filling in this framework in 30 seconds gives you everything you need to answer speaking and writing questions accurately and completely.

TOP STRATEGIES FOR DAILY LIFE READING

Strategy: Read the heading first to identify source and audience. This frames the tone and context for everything that follows.

Strategy: Circle the two reasons immediately. These will be directly challenged or supported in the listening that follows.

Strategy: Note the exact language of the proposal — when speaking or writing, paraphrase it accurately but concisely.

Strategy: Don't spend more than 45 seconds on this passage — the listening carries more weight in integrated tasks.

Strategy: If the passage is a student letter (opinion-based), identify the student's position before the reasons. The listening response will be framed relative to this position.

COMMON MISTAKES TO AVOID

- Mixing up the two reasons given in the announcement
- Spending too much time on the reading when the listening requires full attention
- Summarizing your own opinion about the announcement instead of the student's opinion from the listening
- Forgetting to mention the change itself before discussing the reasons in your integrated response
- Treating the call to action as a third reason — it is a procedural instruction, not a justification
