SPEAKING INTEGRATED TASKS

The TOEFL has three Integrated Speaking tasks (Tasks 2, 3, and 4). Unlike the Independent task, these require you to listen to or read source material and then speak based on that input. You do not give your own opinion — you accurately summarize and synthesize what you heard and read.

OVERVIEW OF THE THREE INTEGRATED TASKS

TASK 2 — CAMPUS SITUATION:
- Read: A campus announcement or proposal (45 to 50 seconds)
- Listen: A student expresses a strong opinion about the announcement (60 to 90 seconds)
- Speak: 60 seconds to summarize the student's opinion and two supporting reasons
- Prep time: 30 seconds

TASK 3 — ACADEMIC CONCEPT + EXAMPLE:
- Read: A short academic definition or concept (45 to 50 seconds)
- Listen: A professor explains the concept using a concrete example or case study
- Speak: 60 seconds to explain the concept AND the professor's example
- Prep time: 30 seconds

TASK 4 — ACADEMIC LECTURE (NO READING):
- Read: Nothing — there is no reading passage
- Listen: An academic lecture presenting a concept with two examples or supporting points
- Speak: 60 seconds to summarize the lecture's main point and two examples
- Prep time: 20 seconds

KEY CONCEPT: In ALL Integrated Speaking tasks, the source content — not your own opinion — is what you speak about. The most penalized error in integrated scoring is adding personal opinions, outside knowledge, or missing the key details from the source. Accuracy and completeness determine your score.

THE SCORING RUBRIC FOR INTEGRATED SPEAKING

Same three dimensions as Independent Speaking (Delivery, Language Use, Topic Development), but the emphasis shifts:

TOPIC DEVELOPMENT in integrated tasks means: Did you accurately and completely convey the key information from the source? Did you connect the parts (reading + listening, or concept + example) correctly?

IMPORTANT: You can have excellent grammar and fluent delivery but still score low on an integrated task if you misrepresent the source material or omit the key example or reasons. Accuracy is the highest priority.

TASK 2 IN DEPTH: CAMPUS SITUATION

THE STRUCTURE OF A STRONG TASK 2 RESPONSE:

Sentence 1: State the announcement's proposal. "The announcement states that [change]."

Sentence 2: State the student's overall opinion. "The student [agrees/disagrees] with this proposal."

Sentence 3: First reason. "First, the student argues that [reason 1]." Include any specific detail mentioned.

Sentence 4: Second reason. "Second, the student believes that [reason 2]." Include any specific detail mentioned.

Optional Sentence 5: One brief connecting detail or implication if time allows.

NOTE: Do not include your own opinion in Task 2. "The student thinks" is correct; "I think" is wrong and will lower your score.

EXAMPLE TASK 2 RESPONSE: "The university has announced that it will close the on-campus gym during winter break for renovations. The student in the conversation disagrees with this plan. First, she argues that many international students remain on campus during winter break and rely on the gym for exercise and stress relief during exam preparation. Second, she points out that the university could schedule renovations during summer break when fewer students are present, making the winter closure unnecessary. For these reasons, she believes the timing of the closure is poorly planned."

TASK 3 IN DEPTH: ACADEMIC CONCEPT AND EXAMPLE

THE STRUCTURE OF A STRONG TASK 3 RESPONSE:

Sentence 1: Define the concept from the reading. "According to the reading, [concept] is [definition]."

Sentence 2: Introduce the professor's example. "The professor illustrates this concept with the example of [subject]."

Sentences 3-4: Explain the example in enough detail to show how it demonstrates the concept. "In this case, [explain the example's key details]."

Sentence 5: Connect the example back to the concept. "This demonstrates [concept] because [connection]."

KEY CONCEPT: The connection between the example and the concept is the heart of Task 3. Many students describe the example correctly but fail to explicitly state how it illustrates the reading's concept. Always close the loop.

EXAMPLE TASK 3 CONCEPT-EXAMPLE BRIDGE: "This is an example of classical conditioning because the dog was learning to associate a neutral stimulus — the bell — with an automatic response, eventually producing that response without the original trigger."

TASK 4 IN DEPTH: ACADEMIC LECTURE SUMMARY

This is the most challenging integrated speaking task because there is no reading to anchor you.

THE STRUCTURE OF A STRONG TASK 4 RESPONSE:

Sentence 1: State the lecture's main point. "The professor discusses [topic], specifically [the concept or process being explained]."

Sentences 2-3: Describe the first example or supporting point with specific detail.

Sentences 4-5: Describe the second example or supporting point with specific detail.

Optional: Brief synthesis if time allows.

NOTE: Many students give only one example because they run out of time. The key is balanced coverage — allocate roughly 20 seconds to the main point and 20 seconds to each example.

NOTE-TAKING FOR INTEGRATED SPEAKING

For ALL integrated speaking tasks, your 30-second preparation time is not enough to plan from scratch. You must take effective notes DURING the reading and listening, and use preparation time to organize what you already have.

READING NOTES: Write the announcement/proposal in one line. Write R1 and R2 for the two reasons.

LISTENING NOTES: Write O for the student's overall opinion. Write S1 and S2 for the two supporting points or examples.

For Task 3: Write DEF for the definition from the reading. Write EX for the example from the lecture, with 2-3 key details.

For Task 4: Write MAIN for the main concept. Write E1 and E2 for the two examples with key details.

IMPORTANT: Your notes don't need full sentences. Trigger words and brief phrases are enough to reconstruct your response during the 30 to 60 seconds of speaking.

LANGUAGE FOR INTEGRATED SPEAKING

ATTRIBUTION PHRASES (always attribute information to its source):
- "The announcement states that..." / "According to the reading..."
- "The student argues that..." / "The man/woman in the conversation believes..."
- "The professor explains that..." / "In the lecture, the professor uses the example of..."
- "This illustrates the concept of..." / "This is an example of..."

TRANSITION PHRASES:
- "First, [source] points out that..." / "Second, [source] mentions that..."
- "Additionally..." / "Furthermore..." / "As a result..."
- "This is significant because..." / "In other words..."

CONNECTION PHRASES (for Task 3):
- "This example demonstrates [concept] because..."
- "According to the reading, [concept] occurs when... The professor shows this through..."
- "Just as the reading explains, the example shows that..."

DELIVERY FOR INTEGRATED SPEAKING

PACE: Integrated tasks require you to deliver more information than Independent tasks in the same time. You may need to speak slightly faster. Practice managing information density.

FLOW: Avoid reading directly from your notes. Notes should trigger your memory, not be read aloud. Reading sounds unnatural and may lower your delivery score.

COMPLETENESS: 60 seconds is enough for two reasons and a position. If you run out of content before time is up, add one detail rather than stopping early or rambling.

REMEMBER: Scorers evaluate 3 dimensions independently. You can have strong delivery and language but low topic development if you misrepresent or omit key content. Prioritize accuracy.

PREPARATION TIME STRATEGY (30 SECONDS)

Step 1 (5 seconds): Scan your notes.
Step 2 (10 seconds): Decide on your opening sentence and state it mentally.
Step 3 (10 seconds): Order your two main points in sequence.
Step 4 (5 seconds): Think of one specific detail to add depth to each point.

Begin speaking at exactly the tone. Do not wait.

TOP STRATEGIES FOR INTEGRATED SPEAKING

Strategy: Always take organized, labeled notes during the reading and listening — not during preparation time.

Strategy: Open with attribution in your first sentence. "The reading states that..." / "The professor discusses..." signals to the scorer that you are on topic immediately.

Strategy: Speak from notes, not memory alone. Glance down, trigger the memory, look up and speak. This is how professionals present, and it sounds natural.

Strategy: Practice Task 4 note-taking with real academic lectures. TED Talks and university lecture recordings are excellent material.

Strategy: After each practice response, evaluate: Did I state the main point? Did I cover both examples? Did I connect them? This self-check trains your monitor.

COMMON MISTAKES TO AVOID

- Adding your own opinion to an integrated task
- Describing only one of the two required examples or reasons
- Failing to connect the lecture example back to the reading concept in Task 3
- Speaking so fast for completeness that pronunciation becomes unclear
- Starting with "Um, so..." instead of a clean, attributed opening sentence
- Stopping before 45 seconds — use all available time for higher topic development scores
