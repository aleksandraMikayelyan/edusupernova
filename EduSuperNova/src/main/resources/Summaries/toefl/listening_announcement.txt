LISTENING ANNOUNCEMENTS AND CAMPUS NOTICES

Announcement-type listening passages are shorter than academic lectures and focus on practical campus information. They simulate real university life: a professor making changes to an assignment, a campus office recording an informational message, or an administrator announcing a policy update.

WHAT TO EXPECT

ANNOUNCEMENT FORMAT: A single speaker (professor, administrator, or campus official) delivers information about a change, event, or policy. Duration: 1 to 2 minutes. Followed by 2 to 3 questions.

CONTENT THEMES:
- Changes to course schedules, office hours, or assignment deadlines
- Campus facility announcements (library closings, gym renovations, IT outages)
- Administrative procedures (registration, financial aid, housing applications)
- Club, event, or volunteer opportunity announcements
- Health and safety notices

KEY CONCEPT: Announcement questions are heavily focused on SPECIFIC DETAILS. Unlike lectures, there is rarely a complex argument to follow. Your goal is to accurately capture WHO is affected, WHAT is changing, WHEN it takes effect, and WHY the change is happening.

THE STANDARD ANNOUNCEMENT STRUCTURE

Most TOEFL announcements follow this formula:

Opening statement: "This is an announcement from the Office of Student Services..." or "I wanted to let you all know about a change to our schedule..." This tells you the source and sets the context.

The main change or information: The core of the message. This is always tested. Write it clearly.

Two reasons or supporting details: Announcements almost always give two justifications or two pieces of supporting information. These are reliable question targets.

Action required or closing: What should the listener do? "Contact the registrar's office," "check the university website," "attend the information session." This closing action is often tested.

IMPORTANT: The two reasons given for a change are almost always the answer to one question. Train yourself to identify them quickly. They are usually signaled by "first" and "second," or "one reason is... another is..."

NOTE-TAKING FOR ANNOUNCEMENTS

Because announcements are short and information-dense, your notes should be structured and efficient:

Write at the top: Source (who is speaking? which office?)
Write: The main change in one line
Write: Reason 1 / Reason 2
Write: Action required

EXAMPLE NOTE STRUCTURE:
Library — closing early (8pm instead of midnight) — this week only
Reason 1: Staff training sessions
Reason 2: System upgrade for new catalog
Action: Use online database or campus computer labs

REMEMBER: Announcements on the TOEFL are designed to mirror real-world listening — the kind of information you need to survive and function at an English-language university. Every detail has a practical purpose.

QUESTION TYPES FOR ANNOUNCEMENTS

MAIN PURPOSE QUESTIONS: "What is the main purpose of this announcement?" The answer identifies the primary message. Wrong answers focus on secondary details.

DETAIL QUESTIONS: "According to the announcement, why is X happening?" These require precise notes about the reasons given. Wrong answers often swap the two reasons or add a reason that was never stated.

INFERENCE QUESTIONS: "What can be inferred from this announcement?" The answer is implied by the information. Common inferences: the change will cause inconvenience, the institution is improving its services, students need to plan ahead.

ATTITUDE/TONE QUESTIONS: "What is the speaker's attitude toward the change?" Administrative announcements are usually neutral or professionally positive. A professor's announcement may carry more personality — apologetic, enthusiastic, or matter-of-fact.

VOCABULARY IN ANNOUNCEMENTS

Semi-formal vocabulary used in campus announcements:

IMPLEMENT: To put a plan into action. "The new policy will be implemented next semester."

POSTPONE / RESCHEDULE: To move to a later time. "The seminar has been postponed until further notice."

MANDATORY / REQUIRED: Compulsory, not optional. "Attendance is mandatory."

WAIVER: An exception or exemption from a rule. "A fee waiver is available for qualifying students."

PROVISIONAL: Temporary or conditional. "This is a provisional schedule and may change."

ELIGIBLE: Meeting the requirements. "Students who qualify are eligible to apply."

PRIOR TO: Before. "Submit your request prior to the deadline."

REGARDING: About or concerning. "Regarding the change to exam format..."

DISTINCTION BETWEEN ANNOUNCEMENTS AND CONVERSATIONS

On the TOEFL, campus announcements differ from campus conversations:
- ANNOUNCEMENTS: One speaker, one-way delivery of information. No back-and-forth dialogue.
- CONVERSATIONS: Two speakers discussing a problem or situation, usually a student and a professor or staff member.

Both types appear in the Listening section, but the question focus differs. Announcements test comprehension of structured information. Conversations test understanding of opinions, concerns, and implicit meaning.

CONNECTING ANNOUNCEMENTS TO READING AND SPEAKING TASKS

Campus announcement readings (not just audio) also appear in Integrated Writing and Speaking. In these tasks:

The reading presents a campus announcement or proposal.
The listening presents a student's reaction (usually two objections or two points of support).

Your task in speaking: Summarize the student's opinion and two reasons, connecting them to the announcement.
Your task in writing: Use only the listening content (do not add your own opinion).

KEY CONCEPT: In Integrated tasks involving campus announcements, you are NOT asked for your personal view. You describe the student's view. Students on TOEFL recordings almost always either fully agree or fully disagree, and they always give exactly two reasons.

TOP STRATEGIES FOR ANNOUNCEMENT LISTENING

Strategy: Identify the speaker's role in the first sentence — this frames everything that follows.

Strategy: Listen for numerical signposts: "first," "second," "one reason," "another issue" — these mark the two key supporting details.

Strategy: If you miss a reason, don't spiral. Look for the closing action — that is always tested and usually comes last.

Strategy: Mentally summarize in one sentence after the announcement ends: "The [source] is announcing [what] because [why]."

Strategy: For integrated tasks, write the announcement's main point on one side of your note paper and the student's two objections on the other side. This keeps the contrast visible.

COMMON MISTAKES TO AVOID

- Confusing the two reasons given (writing reason 2 as reason 1 and vice versa)
- Missing the closing action because attention waned at the end
- Inferring a reason that was never stated in the announcement
- Treating the speaker's neutral delivery as evidence of positive or negative attitude
- In integrated tasks, accidentally including your own opinion instead of the student's
