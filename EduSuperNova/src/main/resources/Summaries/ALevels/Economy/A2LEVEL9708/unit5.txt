INTERNATIONAL TRADE AND EXCHANGE RATES

INTERNATIONAL TRADE THEORY
Absolute advantage: A country produces a good using fewer resources than another country.
Comparative advantage: A country should specialise in the good for which it has the lowest opportunity cost.
  Even if one country is absolutely better at everything, trade is still mutually beneficial.
  Example: If Country A gives up less of Good Y to produce one unit of Good X, it has comparative advantage in X.
Terms of Trade (ToT): Index of export prices relative to import prices.
  ToT = (Index of export prices / Index of import prices) × 100
  Improvement: Export prices rise relative to import prices → country gains from trade.
  Deterioration: Export prices fall relative to import prices → country loses purchasing power.
Gains from trade: Specialisation → economies of scale → greater variety of goods → higher living standards.

BARRIERS TO TRADE (PROTECTIONISM)
Tariff: Tax on imports → raises domestic price → reduces imports, increases domestic production.
Quota: Physical limit on quantity of imports.
Embargoes: Complete ban on trade with a country.
Subsidies to domestic producers: Lowers their costs → more competitive against imports.
Exchange rate manipulation: Deliberately devaluing currency to boost exports.
Arguments for protectionism: Infant industry, strategic industries, dumping, balance of payments, employment.
Arguments against: Retaliation, inefficiency, higher consumer prices, reduced global welfare.

FREE TRADE AND TRADE AGREEMENTS
WTO (World Trade Organisation): Promotes free trade and resolves trade disputes.
Bilateral agreements: Between two countries.
Regional trade blocs:
  Free Trade Area (FTA): No tariffs between members; each keeps own external tariffs (e.g. NAFTA).
  Customs Union: FTA + common external tariff (e.g. EU customs union).
  Common Market: Customs union + free movement of labour and capital (e.g. EU single market).
  Economic Union: Common market + harmonised economic policies (e.g. Eurozone).
Trade creation: Moving from high-cost domestic production to lower-cost imports within the bloc.
Trade diversion: Replacing low-cost world imports with higher-cost bloc imports.

EXCHANGE RATES
Nominal exchange rate: Price of one currency in terms of another.
Real exchange rate: Nominal rate adjusted for relative price levels.
Factors affecting exchange rate: Interest rates, inflation, trade balance, speculation, government policy.

Fixed exchange rate: Government maintains rate at a set level by buying/selling currency.
  Advantage: Certainty for traders, controls inflation.
  Disadvantage: Requires large reserves, loss of monetary policy independence.
Floating exchange rate: Determined by market forces of supply and demand.
  Advantage: Automatic adjustment, no need for reserves, monetary policy independence.
  Disadvantage: Volatility, uncertainty, can be affected by speculation.
Managed float: Central bank intervenes occasionally to smooth fluctuations.

IMPACT OF EXCHANGE RATE CHANGES
Depreciation/devaluation: Currency falls in value.
  Exports cheaper → export demand rises; Imports more expensive → import demand falls.
  J-curve effect: In short run, current account may worsen before improving (contracts adjustment lag).
  Marshall-Lerner condition: Depreciation improves current account if sum of PEDs > 1.
Appreciation/revaluation: Currency rises in value.
  Exports more expensive → export demand falls; Imports cheaper → import demand rises.

BALANCE OF PAYMENTS
Current account: Trade in goods and services + primary income (investment income) + secondary income (transfers).
  Deficit: Imports > exports → currency outflows exceed inflows.
  Surplus: Exports > imports → currency inflows exceed outflows.
Capital and financial account: Investment flows, reserves changes.
Correction of current account deficit:
  Expenditure-switching: Depreciation, tariffs → switch from imports to domestic goods.
  Expenditure-reducing: Deflationary fiscal/monetary policy → reduce income and imports.
