PHONOLOGY AND SOUND
Phoneme: The smallest unit of sound in a language that can change meaning (e.g., /p/ and /b/ in "pat" and "bat").
Grapheme: The written representation of a phoneme; a letter or combination of letters.
Alliteration: The repetition of the same consonant sound at the beginning of closely connected words, used for emphasis or rhythm.
Assonance: The repetition of vowel sounds within nearby words to create a musical or echoing effect.
Sibilance: A form of alliteration using the /s/ or /sh/ sound, often creating a hissing, sinister, or soft effect.
Onomatopoeia: Words that phonetically imitate the sound they describe (e.g., "crash," "whisper," "buzz").
Plosive: A consonant sound made by stopping airflow and releasing it suddenly (/b/, /d/, /g/, /p/, /t/, /k/), often creating a harsh or abrupt effect.
Fricative: A sound produced by forcing air through a narrow channel (e.g., /f/, /v/, /s/, /z/), often creating tension or unease.
Rhyme: The repetition of identical or similar sounds at the end of words, used to create pattern and memorability.
Rhythm: The pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables that gives writing or speech a sense of flow and movement.
Prosody: The patterns of stress and intonation in language; the study of meter, rhythm, and sound in poetry and speech.
Stress: Emphasis placed on a syllable or word in speech, altering meaning or creating impact.

LEXIS AND SEMANTICS
Lexis: The vocabulary of a language; the total stock of words and their meanings.
Semantics: The study of meaning in language, including how words and phrases convey meaning in context.
Denotation: The literal, dictionary definition of a word.
Connotation: The emotional associations, implications, or cultural resonances a word carries beyond its literal meaning.
Synonym: A word with the same or similar meaning to another word.
Antonym: A word with the opposite meaning to another word.
Hyponym: A word that is a specific type within a broader category (e.g., "sparrow" is a hyponym of "bird").
Hypernym/Superordinate: The broader category term under which more specific hyponyms are grouped (e.g., "animal" is the hypernym of "dog," "cat," "bird").
Semantic Field: A group of words that are linked by a shared area of meaning or theme (e.g., words related to war, nature, or religion).
Abstract Noun: A noun referring to a concept, quality, or idea that cannot be physically perceived (e.g., "freedom," "grief," "justice").
Concrete Noun: A noun referring to a physical, tangible object that can be perceived by the senses.
Collocation: Words that naturally and habitually appear together in a language (e.g., "commit a crime," "heavy rain").
Amelioration: A process of semantic change in which a word acquires a more positive meaning over time.
Pejoration: A process of semantic change in which a word acquires a more negative meaning over time.
Euphemism: A mild or indirect word used in place of one that is harsh, blunt, or offensive (e.g., "passed away" for "died").
Dysphemism: The deliberate use of a harsh or offensive term where a neutral or positive one might be expected.
Jargon: Specialised vocabulary used by a particular profession or social group, which may exclude outsiders.
Buzzword: A fashionable word or phrase from a particular field that has gained wide usage, often losing precise meaning.
Neologism: A newly coined word or expression, or an existing word used in a new sense.

GRAMMAR AND SYNTAX
Syntax: The rules governing how words are arranged into sentences and phrases.
Morphology: The study of the structure and form of words, including prefixes, suffixes, and roots.
Morpheme: The smallest grammatical unit of meaning within a word (e.g., "unhappy" contains two morphemes: "un-" and "happy").
Noun Phrase: A phrase with a noun as its head, often expanded by determiners, adjectives, and other modifiers.
Verb Phrase: A phrase with a verb as its head, including auxiliary verbs and their complements.
Simple Sentence: A sentence containing one independent clause with a subject and a verb.
Compound Sentence: A sentence formed by joining two or more independent clauses with a coordinating conjunction (e.g., "and," "but," "or").
Complex Sentence: A sentence containing an independent clause and one or more dependent (subordinate) clauses.
Compound-Complex Sentence: A sentence containing at least two independent clauses and one or more dependent clauses.
Coordination: Joining clauses or phrases of equal grammatical status using coordinating conjunctions.
Subordination: Embedding a dependent clause within a main clause to show a hierarchical relationship of ideas.
Passive Voice: A grammatical construction in which the subject receives the action of the verb (e.g., "The report was written by the committee"), often used to create objectivity or distance.
Active Voice: A grammatical construction in which the subject performs the action (e.g., "The committee wrote the report").
Declarative Sentence: A sentence that makes a statement.
Interrogative Sentence: A sentence that asks a question.
Imperative Sentence: A sentence that issues a command or instruction.
Exclamatory Sentence: A sentence that expresses strong emotion, ending in an exclamation mark.
Anaphora: The deliberate repetition of a word or phrase at the beginning of successive clauses for rhetorical effect.
Epistrophe: The repetition of a word or phrase at the end of successive clauses.
Tricolon: A series of three parallel words, phrases, or clauses, used to create rhythm and emphasise a point.
Fronting: Placing an element other than the subject at the beginning of a sentence for emphasis (e.g., "This I cannot forgive").
Ellipsis: The omission of words that are understood from context, creating economy or informality (e.g., "Going out?" for "Are you going out?").
Deixis: Words that point to something in context — personal (I, you), spatial (here, there), or temporal (now, then).

DISCOURSE AND PRAGMATICS
Discourse: Spoken or written language beyond the level of a single sentence; extended use of language in context.
Pragmatics: The study of how context and social factors influence the interpretation of language beyond its literal meaning.
Speech Act: An utterance that performs an action (e.g., promising, warning, requesting, apologising).
Implicature: The meaning implied by an utterance beyond what is literally said, relying on shared knowledge and context.
Register: The variety of language used in a particular social situation, determined by field, tenor, and mode.
Field: The subject matter or topic of communication.
Tenor: The relationship between the participants in a communication, including their relative status and familiarity.
Mode: The channel of communication — spoken, written, or multimodal.
Cohesion: The way in which elements of a text are linked together to form a unified whole, through devices such as reference, substitution, and connectives.
Coherence: The quality of a text making logical sense as a whole, achieved through consistent topic, argument, and structure.
Topic Sentence: The sentence, usually at the start of a paragraph, that introduces and summarises the paragraph's main idea.
Discourse Marker: A word or phrase used to organise, connect, or signal shifts in spoken or written discourse (e.g., "however," "on the other hand," "right," "anyway").

GRAPHOLOGY AND LAYOUT
Graphology: The study of the visual and presentational features of a text, including typography, layout, and images.
Typography: The style, size, weight, and arrangement of printed text used to create meaning and guide the reader.
Layout: The overall arrangement and organisation of text and images on a page or screen.
Multimodal Text: A text that combines two or more modes of communication, such as written language, images, sound, or gesture.
White Space: Areas of a page or screen deliberately left without text or images, used to guide the eye and create emphasis.
Caption: Text accompanying an image, used to provide context or reinforce the text's message.
Heading/Subheading: Titles used to organise text into sections, signalling the topic of each division to the reader.
