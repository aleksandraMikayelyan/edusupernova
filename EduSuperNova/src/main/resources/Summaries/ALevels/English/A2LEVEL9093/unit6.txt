DIRECTED WRITING AND CRITICAL COMMENTARY

DIRECTED WRITING
Directed writing tasks require transforming or responding to a source text in a new form for a new audience and purpose.
Text transformation: Rewriting a text in a different genre, form, or mode while retaining key content.
  Example: Turning a newspaper report into a speech; adapting an argument into a dialogue.

KEY SKILLS FOR DIRECTED WRITING
Matching register to purpose: Identify the function of the new text (to inform, persuade, entertain, advise).
Adapting tone: Formal for institutional writing; informal for personal; authoritative for persuasive.
Adjusting lexis: Technical vocabulary for specialist audiences; plain language for general readers.
Sentence variety: Short for impact; complex for explanation; rhetorical questions for persuasion.
Structural choices: Opening hook; logical sequencing; effective conclusion.

COMMON DIRECTED WRITING FORMS
Formal letter or email: Proper salutation, structured paragraphs, appropriate sign-off.
Speech or talk: Engaging opening, direct address ("you", "we"), rhetorical devices.
Article or feature: Headline, subheadings, varied sentence length, anecdote as hook.
Report: Objective tone, subheadings, bullet points, recommendations section.
Blog or diary entry: Informal, personal, first-person, conversational asides.
Editorial or opinion piece: Clear stance, evidence and counter-argument, call to action.

RHETORICAL DEVICES FOR PERSUASIVE WRITING
Ethos: Establishing credibility and authority of the speaker/writer.
Pathos: Appealing to the audience's emotions (e.g. anecdote, emotive language).
Logos: Appealing to reason using facts, statistics, logical argument.
Anaphora: Repeating a phrase at the start of successive clauses ("We shall fight on the beaches, we shall fight...").
Antithesis: Contrasting ideas in parallel structure ("To be or not to be...").
Tricolon: Three-part list for emphasis and rhythm ("Life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness").
Rhetorical question: Engages the audience without requiring a spoken answer.
Inclusive pronoun ("we"): Creates solidarity between writer and audience.

CRITICAL COMMENTARY
A commentary analyses your own writing choices: why you made them and what effects they create.
Structure for commentary:
  1. Explain your intended purpose, audience, and form.
  2. Discuss key linguistic choices (lexis, syntax, tone, register).
  3. Link choices to intended effect on the reader.
  4. Acknowledge alternative approaches and why you chose as you did.
Analytical language: "I chose X because...", "The use of Y creates the effect of...", "In order to...".
Avoid simply listing features; explain the effect of each choice.

COMPARING TEXTS
Framework for comparison: Purpose → Audience → Form/Genre → Language choices → Effect.
Similarities and differences: Identify shared and contrasting features.
Contextual factors: Consider when, where, and why each text was produced.
Language level analysis: Phonology, lexis, syntax, discourse structure, pragmatics.
Evaluative judgements: Assess the effectiveness of each text's language in achieving its purpose.

EXAMINATION TECHNIQUE
Reading the question: Identify the required form, audience, purpose, and length.
Planning: Spend 5-10 minutes organising ideas before writing.
Proofreading: Check for consistent register, correct grammar, and clear organisation.
Timing: Allocate time per question according to marks; don't over-write on one section.
