LANGUAGE AND IDENTITY

IDENTITY AND LANGUAGE
Identity: The sense of self, shaped by gender, age, class, ethnicity, profession, and culture.
Language is both a marker of identity and a tool for constructing it.
Social identity theory: People derive part of their identity from the groups they belong to.
Communities of practice: Groups defined by shared activities; members share linguistic norms.

LANGUAGE AND GENDER REVISITED
Language and gender research has moved beyond binary male/female models.
Butler's performativity: Gender is not fixed but performed through repeated behaviours, including language.
Non-binary and gender-neutral language: "They/them" as singular pronouns; avoiding gendered titles.
Language reform: Changing words to reduce sexism (e.g. "chairperson" instead of "chairman").
Marked and unmarked forms: Male forms often unmarked ("actor", "nurse"); female forms marked ("actress", "male nurse").
Generic masculine: Using "he" or "man" to refer to all people; increasingly rejected.

LANGUAGE AND ETHNICITY IN DEPTH
Code-switching: Switching between languages or dialects within a conversation.
  Metaphorical code-switching: Signals a change in topic or relationship (not just practical).
  Situational code-switching: Responds to change in situation or audience.
Identity negotiation: Speakers use language to signal belonging to or distance from groups.
Language death and endangerment: Many minority languages are under threat.
Language revitalisation: Efforts to restore endangered languages (e.g. Welsh, Māori, Irish).

DIGITAL IDENTITY AND ONLINE LANGUAGE
Online language: Creates new norms for written/spoken interaction.
  Asynchronous (e.g. email) vs synchronous (e.g. live chat) communication.
  Netspeak (David Crystal): Language adapted for internet use — abbreviations, emoticons, informal style.
  Emoji: Convey tone, emotion, and nuance in digital communication.
Anonymity online: Can lead to disinhibited language; increased aggression or freedom of expression.
Persona construction: Online profiles allow deliberate identity performance.
Filter bubbles and echo chambers: Algorithms expose users to language confirming existing beliefs.

PROFESSIONAL AND INSTITUTIONAL IDENTITY
Occupational register: Language associated with specific professions (jargon, formality, procedures).
Corporate language: Euphemistic, abstract, nominalisations to project professionalism.
Institutional discourse: Language in schools, hospitals, courts structured by power relations.
Doctor-patient communication: Asymmetric discourse; patients may defer to medical authority.

LANGUAGE, CULTURE AND WORLDVIEW
Linguistic relativity (Sapir-Whorf hypothesis):
  Language shapes thought by providing categories and distinctions.
  Example: Languages with many words for snow (Inuit) may influence perception of snow.
Cultural keywords: Words that capture important cultural concepts (e.g. "Schadenfreude", "ubuntu").
Translation difficulties: Some concepts resist direct translation — evidence of cultural specificity.
Language policy: Government decisions about which languages are official, taught, or supported.
Bilingualism and multilingualism: Increasingly the norm globally; code-switching between languages.
