LANGUAGE VARIATION AND DIVERSITY

VARIETIES OF ENGLISH
Standard English: A prestige variety used in formal contexts; associated with education and power.
  Not a dialect — it can be spoken with any accent.
  Received Pronunciation (RP): The prestige accent of English; associated with BBC English.
Dialect: A regional or social variety distinguished by vocabulary, grammar, and sometimes pronunciation.
Accent: A variety of pronunciation only (not vocabulary or grammar).
Regional dialect: Variation linked to geographical location (e.g. Geordie, Scouse, Yorkshire).
Social dialect (sociolect): Variation linked to social group, class, or identity.

DIMENSIONS OF LANGUAGE VARIATION
Geographical variation: Different regions use different lexis, grammar, and pronunciation.
  Isogloss: A boundary line on a dialect map separating areas using different linguistic features.
Social variation: Language varies with social class, age, gender, ethnicity, and occupation.
Temporal variation: Language changes over time (see Unit 2).
Situational variation (register): Language changes according to context and purpose.

LANGUAGE AND SOCIAL CLASS
Basil Bernstein — Elaborated and Restricted Codes:
  Elaborated code: Explicit, context-independent; associated with middle class.
  Restricted code: Context-dependent, relies on shared knowledge; associated with working class.
William Labov — Social stratification of language:
  Studied New York City pronunciation — class differences in use of post-vocalic /r/.
  Style shifting: Speakers adjust language to context; more formal style in careful speech.
  Prestige: Overt prestige (standard forms); covert prestige (non-standard forms valued within group).

LANGUAGE AND GENDER
Deficit model (Lakoff): Women's language is deficient — hedges, tag questions, polite forms.
Dominance model (Zimmerman & West): Men dominate conversations — interruptions, topic control.
Difference model (Tannen): Men and women use language for different purposes.
  Rapport talk (women): Building relationships.
  Report talk (men): Conveying information, status.
Genderlect: A variety of language associated with a particular gender.
Recent research questions rigid gender categories; context and power matter more.

LANGUAGE AND ETHNICITY
Ethnolect: A dialect associated with a particular ethnic or cultural group.
African American Vernacular English (AAVE): Systematic grammatical rules, e.g. habitual "be".
Code-switching: Moving between languages or dialects depending on context.
Multicultural London English (MLE): Emerging variety influenced by multiple ethnic groups.

LANGUAGE AND AGE
Age-grading: Predictable changes in language use across the lifespan.
Youth language: Slang, neologisms, identity-marking language.
Elderly speech: May retain older forms; slower pace; clarity focus.
Generational differences: Each cohort acquires language norms of their formative years.

PIDGINS AND CREOLES
Pidgin: A simplified contact language developed for communication between groups with no common language.
  No native speakers; limited grammar and vocabulary.
Creole: A pidgin that has been adopted as a first language by a community; fully elaborated grammar.
Decreolisation: Creole becomes more like the prestige variety over time.
